# 1. Mental Model
Inclusion as a process can be likened to navigating a constantly shifting landscape. Imagine you're on a hike, and the terrain keeps changing - new paths emerge, and old ones disappear. You need to continuously adapt and find new ways to traverse the landscape to ensure everyone can move forward together. This ongoing process of adjustment and improvement is similar to how inclusion works.

# 2. Learning Theory & Instructional Design
Inclusion as a process involves recognizing that [[Cultural Capital]] and [[Social Constructivism]] play significant roles in shaping individual experiences. Mechanically, this concept works by fostering an environment where diverse perspectives are acknowledged and valued, allowing for the development of [[Personalized Learning Plans]] that cater to various needs. This process relies on continuous feedback loops and reflective practices to identify areas for improvement. Effective instructional design for inclusion involves recognizing and addressing power dynamics, promoting [[Critical Pedagogy]], and creating opportunities for [[Collaborative Learning]]. By doing so, educators can develop more responsive and adaptive teaching strategies.

# 3. Cognitive Load & Knowledge Gaps
When inclusion as a process is not effectively implemented, it can lead to increased [[Cognitive Load]] on marginalized students, who may struggle to navigate unfamiliar or unsupportive learning environments. This can result in knowledge gaps, as students may not receive the necessary scaffolding to access complex concepts. Boundary conditions, such as limited resources or inadequate teacher training, can exacerbate these issues. Failure states, like tokenism or superficial diversity initiatives, can also undermine the inclusion process. To mitigate these risks, educators must be aware of the potential for [[Implicit Bias]] and [[Microaggressions]], and make a concerted effort to create a culture of ongoing learning and improvement.

## 5. Walkthrough
### Scenario: Implementing Inclusion in a Diverse Classroom

**Step 1: Identify Student Needs**
- A teacher has 25 students with diverse backgrounds, including recent immigrants and students with disabilities.
- Initial assessment reveals gaps in language skills and prior educational experiences.

**Step 2: Apply Cultural Capital and Social Constructivism**
- The teacher incorporates diverse texts and materials that reflect students' backgrounds.
- Strategies like peer mentoring and group projects are used to foster a sense of community.

**Step 3: Develop Personalized Learning Plans**
- The teacher creates plans that account for varied learning styles, language proficiency levels, and prior knowledge.
- Technology is used to provide additional support and resources.

**Step 4: Implement Critical Pedagogy and Collaborative Learning**
- Classroom discussions encourage critical thinking about power dynamics and social justice.
- Students work in groups on projects that require them to apply what they've learned.

**Step 5: Evaluate and Adjust**
- Continuous feedback from students helps identify areas where the inclusion process can be improved.
- Adjustments are made to instructional strategies to better meet student needs.
