# 1. Mental Model
Imagine you're at a school where students of different heights need to watch a basketball game, but the fence around the court is too low to see over. Equality would mean giving every student the same size box to stand on, so they all have the same chance to see over the fence. However, this doesn't account for the fact that students of different heights might still not see the game clearly. Equity, on the other hand, would mean giving each student a box that's tailored to their specific height, so everyone has an equal chance to see the game clearly.

# 2. Learning Theory & Instructional Design
The concepts of equality and equity are deeply rooted in [[Social Constructivist Theory]], which posits that knowledge is constructed through social interactions and experiences. In instructional design, equality refers to providing all learners with the same resources and opportunities, whereas equity involves recognizing and addressing the diverse needs and backgrounds of learners to ensure fair outcomes. This requires [[Universal Design For Learning (Udl)]] principles, which aim to provide multiple means of representation, expression, and engagement. Effective instructional design must balance equality and equity by acknowledging that learners have different starting points and require tailored support to achieve equal outcomes. This is often achieved through [[Differentiated Instruction]], which involves adjusting the content, process, and product of learning to meet individual needs.

# 3. Cognitive Load & Knowledge Gaps
When instructional design prioritizes equality over equity, learners with significant knowledge gaps or different learning needs may experience increased [[Cognitive Load]], as they struggle to keep up with their peers. Conversely, an equitable approach can help mitigate cognitive load by providing targeted support and scaffolding. However, if not implemented carefully, equity-based approaches can create [[Perceived Inequity]], where some learners feel that they are receiving unfair advantages or disadvantages. Moreover, if instructional designers fail to account for the complex interplay between equality and equity, they may inadvertently create [[Knowledge Gaps]] that perpetuate existing inequalities. Therefore, it is crucial to carefully assess learner needs and design instruction that balances equality and equity to promote inclusive and effective learning environments.

## 5. Walkthrough
Suppose we have a class of 20 students with varying levels of math proficiency. The teacher wants to ensure that all students can solve quadratic equations by the end of the unit.

### Step 1: Assess Student Needs
The teacher administers a pre-assessment to identify students who need extra support. The results show that 5 students are struggling with basic algebra, 5 students are on grade level, and 10 students are advanced.

### Step 2: Equality-Based Approach
The teacher decides to provide an equality-based approach, giving all students the same instructional materials and activities. However, this approach may not address the needs of students who are struggling or advanced.

### Step 3: Equity-Based Approach
The teacher decides to implement an equity-based approach, providing targeted support to students who need it. This includes:
* Providing extra algebra support for the 5 struggling students
* Offering advanced challenges for the 10 advanced students
* Maintaining on-grade-level instruction for the 5 students who are already proficient

### Step 4: Implementation
The teacher implements the equity-based approach, adjusting instruction to meet the diverse needs of the students.

### Step 5: Evaluation
The teacher re-assesses student understanding at the end of the unit and finds that:
* The 5 struggling students have made significant progress and can now solve basic quadratic equations
* The 10 advanced students have mastered quadratic equations and are ready for more challenging topics
* The 5 on-grade-level students have maintained their proficiency

By taking an equity-based approach, the teacher was able to address the diverse needs of the students and ensure that all students had an equal chance to succeed.
