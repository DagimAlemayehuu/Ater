# 1. Mental Model
Imagine you're a manager at a bustling restaurant, and one of your servers has a broken leg. To ensure they can still do their job, you adjust their tasks so they can focus on taking orders and managing tables from a seated position. This way, they can still contribute without overexerting themselves. Similarly, workplace accommodations involve making adjustments to a job or work environment to enable an employee with a disability to perform their job effectively.

# 2. Learning Theory & Instructional Design
Workplace accommodations operate through a framework that involves assessing an employee's needs and implementing adjustments to their job duties, work environment, or work schedule. This process relies on [[Job_Analysis]] to identify the essential functions of a job and determine how accommodations can be made without compromising those functions. The [[Americans_With_Disabilities_Act]] (ADA) and [[Section_504]] of the Rehabilitation Act provide the legal framework for workplace accommodations, requiring employers to engage in an [[Interactive_Process]] with employees to determine suitable accommodations.

# 3. Cognitive Load & Knowledge Gaps
When workplace accommodations are not properly implemented, it can lead to increased [[Cognitive_Load]] on employees who may struggle to adapt to their new work environment. If an employee's needs are not accurately assessed, it can result in [[Inadequate_Training]] and [[Performance_Gaps]], ultimately affecting their ability to perform their job duties. Moreover, a lack of clear policies and procedures for providing accommodations can create [[Organizational_Ambiguity]], making it challenging for employees and managers to navigate the accommodation process. Effective workplace accommodations require a thorough understanding of an employee's needs and the implementation of tailored solutions to mitigate these risks.

## 5. Walkthrough
Here's a step-by-step scenario applying the concept of workplace accommodations:

An employee, John, has been working as a data analyst for a company. Recently, he was diagnosed with a repetitive strain injury that prevents him from typing for extended periods. The company wants to provide John with a workplace accommodation to enable him to continue performing his job effectively.

1. **Job Analysis**: The HR department conducts a job analysis to identify the essential functions of John's job as a data analyst. They determine that John's primary responsibilities include data analysis, report writing, and presenting findings to stakeholders.

2. **Needs Assessment**: The HR department meets with John to assess his needs and determine the specific accommodations required. John explains that he needs to reduce his typing time and avoid repetitive tasks.

3. **Accommodation Identification**: Based on the job analysis and needs assessment, the HR department identifies potential accommodations, including:
	* Providing a voice-to-text software to reduce typing time
	* Hiring a part-time assistant to help with data entry
	* Adjusting John's work schedule to allow for more frequent breaks

4. **Interactive Process**: The HR department engages in an interactive process with John to discuss the potential accommodations and determine the most suitable solution. John provides feedback on the options and suggests a combination of the voice-to-text software and adjusted work schedule.

5. **Implementation and Evaluation**: The HR department implements the agreed-upon accommodations and monitors John's progress. After a few weeks, they evaluate the effectiveness of the accommodations and make any necessary adjustments.
