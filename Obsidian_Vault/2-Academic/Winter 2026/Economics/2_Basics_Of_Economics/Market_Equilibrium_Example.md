## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a school bake sale, where you're selling delicious cookies. The price of each cookie is like a seesaw - if it's too high, not many kids want to buy it, but if it's too low, you won't make enough money. Let's say you want to sell cookies for 50 cents each, but only 5 kids want to buy them at that price. If you lower the price to 25 cents, 10 kids want to buy them, but you're not making as much money. If you raise the price to 75 cents, only 2 kids want to buy them. The magic happens when you set the price at 40 cents and exactly 8 kids want to buy them - that's the market equilibrium! You're making just the right amount of money, and the kids are getting just the right number of cookies. The price and number of cookies sold are balanced, like a seesaw in perfect balance, and that's why it's called market equilibrium.

## 2. Micro Theory

In microeconomics, a **Market Equilibrium Example** illustrates the point at which the quantity of a good or service that suppliers are willing to sell (**supply**) equals the quantity that buyers are willing to buy (**demand**). This equilibrium is a fundamental concept in understanding how markets allocate resources.

The market equilibrium is determined by the intersection of the **Market Demand Curve** and the **Market Supply Curve**. The **Market Demand Curve** represents the aggregate demand of all consumers in the market, which is typically downward sloping, reflecting the **Law of Demand**. This law states that, ceteris paribus (all else being equal, as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption), as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases. The demand side can also be represented by a **Demand Schedule**, a table showing the quantity demanded at different price levels, or a **Demand Function**, which mathematically expresses the relationship between the quantity demanded and its determinants, including price, income, and prices of related goods.

On the supply side, the **Law of Supply** posits that, ceteris paribus, as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied also increases. This relationship can be depicted by a **Market Supply Curve**, which is usually upward sloping. The supply side can also be described by a **Supply Schedule** or a **Supply Function**, detailing the quantity supplied at various price levels.

To calculate the market equilibrium price and quantity, one must equate the **Market Demand** function with the **Market Supply** function. For simplicity, let's assume a linear **Demand Function**: Qd = a - bp, where Qd is the quantity demanded, p is the price, and a and b are constants. Similarly, let's assume a linear **Supply Function**: Qs = c + dp, where Qs is the quantity supplied, and c and d are constants.

At market equilibrium, Qd = Qs. Therefore, we set a - bp = c + dp. Solving for p gives us the equilibrium price (pe). 

For instance, if the demand function is Qd = 100 - 2p and the supply function is Qs = 50 + 3p, setting them equal to each other yields: 100 - 2p = 50 + 3p. Solving for p, we get 50 = 5p, which results in pe = 10. Substituting pe back into either the demand or supply function to find the equilibrium quantity (qe), we use Qd = 100 - 2*10 = 80, or Qs = 50 + 3*10 = 80.

Thus, the market equilibrium price (pe) is $10, and the equilibrium quantity (qe) is 80 units. This **Market Equilibrium Example** demonstrates how market forces interact to determine prices and quantities in a competitive market, assuming [[Market_Demand]] and Market Supply curves intersect.

The stability of this equilibrium can be analyzed through the lens of **Surplus and Shortage**. If the market price is above pe, a surplus occurs because quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. If the price is below pe, a shortage results because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Market forces tend to push the price towards pe, where there is no surplus or shortage.

Changes in **Market Equilibrium** can occur due to shifts in the demand or supply curves. For example, a **Shift in Supply Curve** can result from [[Technological_Advancement]] or a [[Change_In_Production_Costs]], affecting the supply side. On the demand side, changes can be influenced by factors such as [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], and [[Cross_Price_Elasticity]], especially for [[Normal_Goods]], [[Inferior_Goods]], [[Substitute_Goods]], and [[Complementary_Goods]].

The analysis of **Market Equilibrium Example** provides insights into how various factors influence market outcomes and how markets adjust to changes in economic conditions. Understanding these dynamics, including the role of elasticities such as Price Elasticity Of Supply and the factors affecting [[Elasticity_Of_Supply]], like [[Length_And_Complexity_Of_Production]], [[Mobility_And_Availability_Of_Factors]], and [[Time_To_Respond]], is crucial for both policymakers and businesses.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The market equilibrium example assumes a perfectly competitive market with a linear demand and supply curve, which may not accurately represent real-world complexities. A significant limitation arises when dealing with externalities, as the equilibrium price and quantity may not account for social costs or benefits. Additionally, the example often relies on the assumption of perfect information, which may not hold in cases where buyers or sellers have incomplete knowledge about market conditions. Furthermore, the model may not accommodate cases of market power, where firms can influence prices, or sticky prices, where prices do not adjust immediately to changes in market conditions. Moreover, the example often neglects to consider the role of government interventions, such as taxes or subsidies, which can impact market equilibrium. Lastly, the model assumes a static environment, neglecting the potential effects of dynamic changes in market conditions over time.

The market equilibrium condition is where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied: $Q_d = Q_s$. Substituting the demand and supply functions into this condition gives us $100 - 2P = 50 + 3P$.

## 3: Solve for the Equilibrium Price

Now, we solve for $P$:
$100 - 2P = 50 + 3P$.
Rearranging the equation to solve for $P$:
$100 - 50 = 50P + 2P$,
$50 = 5P$,
$P = 10$.

## 4: Solve for the Equilibrium Quantity

To find the equilibrium quantity, we substitute the equilibrium price back into either the demand or supply function. Using the demand function:
$Q_d = 100 - 2(10) = 100 - 20 = 80$.
Alternatively, using the supply function:
$Q_s = 50 + 3(10) = 50 + 30 = 80$.
Thus, the equilibrium quantity is $80$.
