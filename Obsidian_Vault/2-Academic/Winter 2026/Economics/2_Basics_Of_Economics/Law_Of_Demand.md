## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at your favorite ice cream shop, and they sell super yummy cones for $1 each. If they charge $1, you might want to buy 5 cones, but if they suddenly raise the price to $2, you might only want to buy 2 cones because it's too expensive. On the other hand, if they lower the price to 50 cents, you might want to buy 10 cones because it's a sweet deal! This is kind of like what happens with the Law of Demand - when the price of something (like ice cream) goes up, people want to buy less of it, and when the price goes down, people want to buy more of it.

## 2. Micro Theory

The [[Law_Of_Demand]] is a fundamental concept in microeconomics that describes the inverse relationship between the price of a commodity and its quantity demanded. This relationship is typically expressed as a negative correlation between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, ceteris paribus, or [[Ceteris_Paribus]], which implies that all other influencing factors remain constant.

Mathematically, the [[Law_Of_Demand]] can be represented through a [[Demand_Function]], which expresses the quantity demanded of a good as a function of its price, along with other factors that influence demand. A general form of the demand function can be written as Qd = f(P, I, T, Ps, Pc), where Qd is the quantity demanded, P is the price of the good, I is the consumer's income, T represents tastes and preferences, Ps is the price of substitute goods, and Pc is the price of complementary goods.

The [[Demand_Schedule]], which is a tabular representation of the various quantities of a good that consumers are willing and able to buy at different price levels, illustrates the [[Law_Of_Demand]]. When graphically represented, the demand schedule becomes a [[Demand_Curve]], which typically slopes downward from left to right, visually demonstrating the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

The [[Market_Demand]] and [[Market_Demand_Curve]] are the aggregate representations of individual demand functions and curves, summing up the quantities demanded by all consumers in the market at each price level.

The demand side, as described by the [[Law_Of_Demand]], interacts with the supply side, governed by the [[Law_Of_Supply]], to achieve [[Market_Equilibrium]], where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.

Any shift in the [[Demand_Curve]] or [[Market_Demand_Curve]], due to changes in determinants such as income, tastes, or prices of related goods, can lead to a new equilibrium. Similarly, shifts in the Supply Curve, caused by factors like [[Technological_Advancement]], [[Change_In_Production_Costs]], affect market equilibrium.

Understanding the [[Law_Of_Demand]] and its implications on market dynamics, including the effects of shifts in demand and supply, and concepts like [[Surplus_And_Shortage]], provides valuable insights into how markets operate and respond to various economic stimuli.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The Law of Demand has several limitations and edge cases, including the possibility of a Giffen good, which is a rare and inferior good whose demand increases as its price rises, thus violating the law. Additionally, in cases of extreme necessity or emergency, such as during a war or famine, people may be forced to buy more of a good even if its price rises, again contradicting the law. Furthermore, the law assumes that the income effect and substitution effect work in the same direction, but in the case of inferior goods, these effects can work in opposite directions, leading to an exception. Lastly, the law also assumes that the consumer has a rational behavior, but in reality, consumers may exhibit irrational behavior, such as buying more of a good due to its prestige or snob appeal, rather than its utility, thus limiting the applicability of the Law of Demand.

The Law of Demand states that the price of a commodity and its quantity demanded are inversely related.

**Step 2: Establish the Demand Function**
The demand function can be represented as Qd = f(P), where Qd is the quantity demanded and P is the price of the good. Since the source text does not provide additional variables, we will assume ceteris paribus, implying that all other influencing factors remain constant.
