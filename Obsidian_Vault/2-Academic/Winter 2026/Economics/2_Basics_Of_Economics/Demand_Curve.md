## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a candy store and you love their super yummy chocolates. The store owner, Mr. Sweet, normally sells each chocolate for $1. You really want 5 chocolates, but if Mr. Sweet increases the price to $2, you might think twice and only want 3 chocolates. But if he lowers the price to 50 cents, you might get excited and want 8 chocolates! That's kind of like a demand curve. It shows how many chocolates (or any product) people want to buy at different prices. When the price is high, people want to buy less (like 3 chocolates at $2), and when the price is low, people want to buy more (like 8 chocolates at 50 cents). The demand curve helps us see this relationship between price and how much people want to buy something.

## 2. Statistical Model

The demand curve is a fundamental concept in economics, specifically in microeconomics, which illustrates the inverse relationship between the price of a commodity and the quantity demanded by consumers over a specified time period. This relationship is often referred to as the [[Law_Of_Demand]], which states that, ceteris paribus (all else being equal), as the price of a commodity increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa. The [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption is crucial here, as it implies that other factors that could influence demand, such as consumer income, tastes, and the prices of related goods, remain constant.

The demand curve is typically derived from a [[Demand_Schedule]], which is a table that shows the quantities of a commodity demanded at different price levels. When plotted on a graph, with the price on the vertical axis and the quantity demanded on the horizontal axis, the resulting curve is the [[Demand_Curve]]. This curve slopes downward from left to right, reflecting the negative relationship between price and quantity demanded.

Mathematically, the demand curve can be represented by a [[Demand_Function]], which expresses the quantity demanded as a function of the price of the commodity, as well as other factors that influence demand. For a given market, the [[Market_Demand]] curve is the horizontal summation of the individual demand curves of all consumers in the market, resulting in a [[Market_Demand_Curve]].

The responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in price is measured by the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]]. This concept is essential in understanding how changes in price affect the quantity demanded and, consequently, the total revenue of producers. Related concepts, such as [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] and [[Cross_Price_Elasticity]], provide further insights into how demand responds to changes in consumer income and the prices of related goods, respectively.

Goods can be classified based on their elasticity and relationship to other goods. For instance, [[Normal_Goods]] exhibit a positive income elasticity of demand, while [[Inferior_Goods]] exhibit a negative income elasticity. [[Substitute_Goods]] and [[Complementary_Goods]] are related through their cross-price elasticity, where an increase in the price of one good leads to an increase in the demand for its substitute and a decrease in the demand for its complement.

The demand curve is also influenced by factors such as [[Technological_Advancement]] and [[Change_In_Production_Costs]], which can affect the supply side of the market. However, the [[Law_Of_Supply]] and the [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]] are more directly related to the supply side of the market. The interaction between the demand and supply curves determines the [[Market_Equilibrium]], where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Deviations from this equilibrium result in [[Surplus_And_Shortage]].

Understanding the demand curve and its determinants is crucial for businesses and policymakers to make informed decisions. For instance, analyzing the effects of a Shift In Demand And Supply can help predict how changes in market conditions will impact prices and quantities. Moreover, concepts like [[Elasticity_Of_Supply]], [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], [[Point_And_Arc_Method]], [[Types_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], [[Length_And_Complexity_Of_Production]], [[Mobility_And_Availability_Of_Factors]], and [[Time_To_Respond]] provide a more nuanced view of the supply side and its responsiveness to changes in market conditions.

To illustrate these concepts, consider a [[Market_Equilibrium_Example]], where the demand and supply curves intersect to determine the equilibrium price and quantity. Shifts in either curve can lead to changes in the equilibrium, demonstrating the dynamic nature of markets and the importance of understanding both demand and supply in analyzing market outcomes.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The demand curve assumes a linear relationship between price and quantity demanded, which may not always hold true in reality, as the relationship can be non-linear and influenced by various factors such as consumer behavior, income effects, and substitution effects; moreover, the demand curve is typically estimated using historical data, which may not accurately capture changes in consumer preferences, technological advancements, or external shocks, leading to potential inaccuracies in predicting future demand; additionally, the curve is often based on the ceteris paribus assumption, which implies that all other factors affecting demand remain constant, a condition rarely met in real-world scenarios, thereby limiting the curve's ability to accurately forecast demand responses to price changes.

### Demand Curve Representation

The demand curve can be represented using the following LaTeX equation:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Price (P)} & \text{Quantity Demanded (Q)} \\
\hline
P_1 & Q_1 \\
P_2 & Q_2 \\
\vdots & \vdots \\
P_n & Q_n \\
\end{array}
$$

However, to accurately depict the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded in a more conventional and interpretable form, we use:

$$
Q = f(P) \implies Q = a - bP
$$

where:
- $Q$ is the quantity demanded,
- $P$ is the price of the commodity,
- $a$ and $b$ are constants, with $b > 0$ to reflect the downward slope.

### Explanation

The provided LaTeX equation represents a simple linear demand curve, where the quantity demanded decreases as the price increases, illustrating the law of demand. This equation is a fundamental tool in microeconomics for analyzing consumer behavior and understanding how changes in price affect the quantity demanded of a commodity.

## 5. Walkthrough

### 5-Step Technical Walkthrough of Demand Curve

#### Step 1: Define the Demand Schedule
The demand schedule is a table that lists the quantities of a commodity that consumers are willing to buy at different price levels. For example, consider a demand schedule for **Apple iPhones** over a specified time period (e.g., one month).

| Price per iPhone | Quantity Demanded |
|------------------|-------------------|
| $800             | 100               |
| $700             | 150               |
| $600             | 200               |

#### Step 2: Identify the Variables
- **Price per iPhone**: The price of the commodity (Apple iPhones) on the vertical axis.
- **Quantity Demanded**: The quantity of iPhones demanded on the horizontal axis.

#### Step 3: Plot the Demand Curve
Plot the data from the demand schedule on a graph:
- The vertical axis (y-axis) represents the **Price per iPhone**.
- The horizontal axis (x-axis) represents the **Quantity Demanded**.

Example points to plot:
- At $800, quantity demanded is 100 iPhones.
- At $700, quantity demanded is 150 iPhones.
- At $600, quantity demanded is 200 iPhones.

#### Step 4: Analyze the Slope of the Demand Curve
The demand curve slopes downward from left to right. This means as the price of the iPhone decreases, the quantity demanded increases. For instance:
- When the price drops from $800 to $700, the quantity demanded increases from 100 to 150 iPhones.
- When the price drops from $700 to $600, the quantity demanded increases from 150 to 200 iPhones.

#### Step 5: Interpret the Demand Curve
The downward-sloping demand curve illustrates the **Law of Demand**, which states that, ceteris paribus (all else being equal), as the price of a commodity increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa. This curve assumes that other factors influencing demand, such as consumer income, tastes, and prices of related goods, remain constant.
