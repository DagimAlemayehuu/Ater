## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're a lemonade stand owner, and you want to know how easily you can change the amount of lemonade you sell when the price changes. Let's say you normally sell 10 cups of lemonade for $1 each. If you raise the price to $2, you might be able to make 20 cups of lemonade because you want to take advantage of the higher price. The formula for Price Elasticity of Supply (𝑬𝑺) helps figure out how much more or less lemonade you can make when the price changes. It's like a special calculator that looks at how much the amount of lemonade you sell changes (𝑸) compared to how much the price changes (𝑷). The formula is: 𝑬𝑺 = % change in quantity supplied / % change in price. For example, if you sell 100% more lemonade (from 10 to 20 cups) when the price increases by 100% (from $1 to $2), your 𝑬𝑺 would be 1 (100%/100%). This means you can easily change the amount of lemonade you sell when the price changes, and your lemonade stand is very responsive to price changes!

## 2. Micro Theory

The **Price Elasticity of Supply Formula**, denoted as \(E_S\), is a measure used in microeconomics to assess the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a good to a change in its price, while holding all other factors constant, as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. The formula for price elasticity of supply is:

\[ E_S = \frac{\% \text{ change in quantity supplied}}{\% \text{ change in price}} \]

This concept is closely related to the [[Law_Of_Supply]], which states that, ceteris paribus, an increase in the price of a good will lead to an increase in the quantity supplied. The price elasticity of supply, however, quantifies this relationship, providing a precise measure of how suppliers respond to price changes.

The [[Demand_Schedule]] and [[Demand_Curve]] are tools used to analyze demand side of the market, while the supply side is analyzed through the Supply Schedule and Supply Curve. The price elasticity of supply can be understood in conjunction with [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], which measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in price.

The calculation of \(E_S\) often employs the [[Point_And_Arc_Method]]. The point method calculates elasticity at a specific point on the supply curve, using the formula:

\[ E_S = \frac{dQ}{dP} \times \frac{P}{Q} \]

where \(dQ/dP\) is the derivative of the Supply Function with respect to price, \(P\) is the price, and \(Q\) is the quantity supplied.

The arc method, on the other hand, measures elasticity over a range of prices and quantities, using the formula:

\[ E_S = \frac{\Delta Q / Q_{avg}}{\Delta P / P_{avg}} \]

where \(\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta P\) are changes in quantity and price, respectively, and \(Q_{avg}\) and \(P_{avg}\) are the average quantities and prices over the interval.

The [[Types_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]] include elastic supply, inelastic supply, and unit elastic supply, depending on whether \(E_S\) is greater than, less than, or equal to 1, respectively. The determinants of elasticity of supply, such as [[Length_And_Complexity_Of_Production]], [[Mobility_And_Availability_Of_Factors]], and [[Time_To_Respond]], influence how easily suppliers can adjust their production levels in response to price changes.

Understanding \(E_S\) is crucial for analyzing [[Market_Equilibrium]], [[Surplus_And_Shortage]], and the [[Effects_Of_Shift_In_Demand_And_Supply]]. Factors such as [[Technological_Advancement]] and [[Change_In_Production_Costs]] can shift the supply curve and affect the elasticity of supply.

The concept of price elasticity of supply provides valuable insights into how suppliers respond to market conditions, complementing the analysis of [[Market_Demand]] and [[Market_Demand_Curve]]. For [[Normal_Goods]], [[Inferior_Goods]], [[Substitute_Goods]], and [[Complementary_Goods]], understanding the elasticity of supply in conjunction with [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], [[Cross_Price_Elasticity]], and [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] offers a comprehensive view of market dynamics.

In conclusion, the Price Elasticity of Supply Formula is a fundamental tool in microeconomics that quantifies the responsiveness of suppliers to changes in price, assuming [[Ceteris_Paribus]]. Its calculation and interpretation are essential for understanding market behavior and the effects of various economic factors on supply.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The Price Elasticity of Supply (PES) formula, \(E_S = \frac{\% \ change \ in \ quantity \ supplied}{\% \ change \ in \ price}\), has several limitations and edge cases. For instance, it assumes that the relationship between price and quantity supplied is linear, which may not always be the case, particularly over large price ranges. Additionally, the formula is sensitive to the choice of units and the time period considered, as supply responses can vary significantly in the short run versus the long run. Furthermore, the PES formula may not accurately capture supply behavior at extreme price levels, such as near-zero prices or prices that are very high, where supply responses may be atypical. Moreover, the formula does not account for external factors that can influence supply, such as changes in technology, input prices, or government policies, which can lead to shifts in the supply curve rather than movements along it. Finally, the PES formula can be problematic when applied to markets with non-standard supply functions, such as those with quantity rationing or non-price allocation mechanisms.

## 5. Walkthrough

## Step 1: Define the Price Elasticity of Supply Formula

The Price Elasticity of Supply Formula is given by \( E_S = \frac{\% \text{ change in quantity supplied}}{\% \text{ change in price}} \), which quantifies the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a good to a change in its price.

## Step 2: Identify the Components of the Formula

The formula requires two key components:
1. Percentage change in quantity supplied.
2. Percentage change in price.

## 3: Calculate Percentage Change in Quantity Supplied

The percentage change in quantity supplied is calculated as \( \frac{\Delta Q}{Q} \times 100\% \), where \( \Delta Q \) is the change in quantity supplied and \( Q \) is the original quantity supplied.

## 4: Calculate Percentage Change in Price

The percentage change in price is calculated as \( \frac{\Delta P}{P} \times 100\% \), where \( \Delta P \) is the change in price and \( P \) is the original price.

## 5: Apply the Formula

To find \( E_S \), divide the percentage change in quantity supplied by the percentage change in price:
\[ E_S = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{Q} \times 100\%}{\frac{\Delta P}{P} \times 100\%} \]
Simplifying, we get:
\[ E_S = \frac{\Delta Q / Q}{\Delta P / P} \]
This formula provides a measure of the price elasticity of supply, indicating how responsive suppliers are to changes in price.
