## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you love buying ice cream cones from your favorite shop. One day, the shop owner raises the price from $2 to $3. If you and all your friends still buy just as many cones as before, but just grumble a bit about the higher price, that means demand is "inelastic" - like a rubber band that's hard to stretch. But, if the price increase makes you and your friends decide to buy only half as many cones, or even switch to buying popsicles instead, that means demand is "elastic" - like a big stretchy rubber band that easily changes shape. The more you and your friends change your buying habits when the price changes, the more "elastic" the demand is!

## 2. Micro Theory

The concept of Price Elasticity Of Demand (PED) is a fundamental microeconomic tool used to quantify the degree of responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its price, while holding all other influencing factors constant, as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption. It is defined as the percentage change in the quantity demanded of a good in response to a percentage change in its price.

Mathematically, PED can be expressed as:

PED = (Percentage change in quantity demanded) / (Percentage change in price)

 PED = (∆Q/Q) / (∆P/P)

Where ∆Q and ∆P represent changes in quantity and price respectively.

The PED is intricately linked with the [[Law_Of_Demand]], which states that, ceteris paribus, an increase in the price of a good leads to a decrease in the quantity demanded, and vice versa. A PED value can be positive or negative; however, by convention, it is usually reported as a positive value. 

PED values can be interpreted as follows: if PED is greater than 1, demand is said to be elastic, implying that a price change leads to a more than proportionate change in the quantity demanded. Conversely, if PED is less than 1, demand is inelastic, indicating that a price change results in a less than proportionate change in the quantity demanded. A PED value of 1 signifies unit elasticity, where the change in price leads to an equal proportionate change in the quantity demanded.

The PED is influenced by various factors including the [[Demand_Schedule]] and the [[Demand_Curve]], which graphically represent the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. The [[Demand_Function]], which mathematically expresses this relationship, also plays a crucial role in determining PED.

PED can be measured using the [[Point_And_Arc_Method]]. The point method measures PED at a specific point on the demand curve, while the arc method measures PED over a range of prices.

The concept of PED has significant implications for businesses and policymakers. For instance, firms producing goods with elastic demand, such as [[Substitute_Goods]], may experience a substantial change in sales volume in response to a price change. On the other hand, firms producing goods with inelastic demand, such as [[Normal_Goods]] with few substitutes, may not experience a significant change in sales volume.

Furthermore, PED is related to other elasticity measures, including [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] and [[Cross_Price_Elasticity]], which capture the responsiveness of demand to changes in income and prices of related goods, respectively.

The interplay between PED and [[Market_Demand]], which represents the total quantity demanded of a good by all consumers in a market, and the [[Market_Demand_Curve]], which graphically represents this relationship, is crucial in understanding market dynamics.

Ultimately, PED plays a vital role in determining [[Market_Equilibrium]], where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Changes in PED can lead to [[Surplus_And_Shortage]] situations, which can have significant effects on market outcomes.

In conclusion, the Price Elasticity Of Demand is a vital microeconomic concept that provides valuable insights into the responsiveness of demand to changes in price, with far-reaching implications for businesses, policymakers, and markets.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The price elasticity of demand has several limitations, including the assumption that changes in price are the sole cause of changes in demand, overlooking the impact of other influential factors such as income, tastes, and prices of related goods. Additionally, the measure is sensitive to the specific time period considered, as demand may be more elastic in the long run than in the short run. Furthermore, the calculation of price elasticity of demand assumes a linear demand curve, which may not accurately represent real-world market behavior, and it also fails to account for the distribution of income among consumers, which can affect the elasticity of demand. Moreover, at the extreme ends of the demand curve, such as with perfectly elastic or perfectly inelastic demand, the concept of price elasticity of demand becomes less meaningful. Lastly, the measure can be affected by the choice of units used to express price and quantity, which can lead to inconsistencies in comparisons across different markets or products.

## 5. Walkthrough

## Step 1: Define the Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) Formula

The PED formula is given by: PED = (Percentage change in quantity demanded) / (Percentage change in price). Mathematically, this can be expressed as PED = (∆Q/Q) / (∆P/P).

## Step 2: Understand the Interpretation of PED Values

PED values can be interpreted based on their magnitude. If PED > 1, demand is elastic. If PED < 1, demand is inelastic. If PED = 1, demand is unit elastic.

## 3: Apply the PED Formula with an Example

Suppose the price of WTI Crude Oil increases from $50 to $60 (∆P = $10) and the quantity demanded decreases from 1000 barrels to 800 barrels (∆Q = -200 barrels). The initial price (P) is $50, and the initial quantity (Q) is 1000 barrels.

## 4: Calculate Percentage Changes

Calculate the percentage change in quantity demanded: (∆Q/Q) = (-200 / 1000) = -0.2 or -20%.
Calculate the percentage change in price: (∆P/P) = ($10 / $50) = 0.2 or 20%.

## 5: Calculate PED

Substitute the percentage changes into the PED formula: PED = (-20% / 20%) = -1. By convention, PED is reported as a positive value, so PED = 1.

The final answer is 1.
