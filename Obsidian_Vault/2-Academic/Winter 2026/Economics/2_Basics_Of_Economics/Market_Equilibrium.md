## 2. Micro Theory

The concept of **Market Equilibrium** is a fundamental notion in microeconomics, which occurs when the quantity demanded (𝑸𝒅) equals the quantity supplied (𝑸𝒑) of a particular good or service in a market. This equilibrium is achieved when the demand and supply curves intersect, resulting in a stable price and quantity.

The **Market Demand** curve, which represents the aggregate demand of all consumers in the market, is typically downward sloping, as described by the [[Law_Of_Demand]]. This implies that as the price of the good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, ceteris paribus (all other things being equal), as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption. The demand curve can be represented by a [[Demand_Function]], which expresses the relationship between the quantity demanded and various factors that influence demand, such as price, income, and prices of related goods.

On the other hand, the **Market Supply** curve, which represents the aggregate supply of all producers in the market, is typically upward sloping, as described by the [[Law_Of_Supply]]. This implies that as the price of the good increases, the quantity supplied also increases. The supply curve can be influenced by various factors, such as [[Technological_Advancement]] and [[Change_In_Production_Costs]], which can cause a [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]].

The market equilibrium is achieved when the demand and supply curves intersect, resulting in a market equilibrium price (𝑃∗) and quantity (𝑄∗), where 𝑸𝒅 = 𝑸𝒔. At this point, the number of buyers willing to purchase the good at the prevailing price equals the number of sellers willing to sell the good at that price.

The stability of the market equilibrium depends on the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] and Price Elasticity Of Supply, which measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded and supplied to changes in price. For normal goods, an increase in income leads to an increase in demand, while for inferior goods, an increase in income leads to a decrease in demand. Additionally, the [[Cross_Price_Elasticity]] of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in the prices of related goods, such as [[Substitute_Goods]] and [[Complementary_Goods]].

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

Market equilibrium, as represented by the intersection of demand (𝑸𝒅) and supply (𝑸𝒔) curves, has several limitations. It assumes that all market participants have perfect information, which is rarely the case in reality. Additionally, it relies on the ceteris paribus assumption, which holds other factors constant, but in reality, external factors such as changes in consumer preferences, technological advancements, or government policies can influence market equilibrium. Furthermore, market equilibrium does not account for market power imbalances, information asymmetry, or externalities, which can lead to market failures. Moreover, it assumes that markets clear instantly, which is not always the case, and that prices are flexible, which may not be true in the presence of price stickiness or rigidities. Lastly, market equilibrium does not consider the possibility of non-market equilibrium states, such as rationing or queuing, which can occur in certain markets.
