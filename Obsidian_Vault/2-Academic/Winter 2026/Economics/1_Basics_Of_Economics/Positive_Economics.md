## 1. Mental Model

Imagine a librarian who catalogues books based on their current availability and popularity. This librarian is like a positive economist, describing the current state of the library's collection without making value judgments about which books should be there. The librarian's task is to provide accurate information about what's currently on the shelves, not to decide what should or shouldn't be there. Similarly, positive economists focus on describing the economy as it is, without making subjective judgments about how it should be.

## 2. Foundational Concept

Positive economics is a branch of economics [[Branches_Of_Economics]] that focuses on describing the world as it is, rather than how it should be. It involves the use of [[Inductive_Reasoning]] to analyze data and draw conclusions about economic phenomena. Positive economics seeks to answer questions such as what was, what is, and what will be, based on empirical evidence. This approach is often contrasted with [[Normative_Economics]], which involves making subjective judgments about economic policies or outcomes. In the context of [[Economic_Systems]], positive economics provides a factual basis for understanding how economies function, which is essential for addressing [[Basic_Economic_Questions]]. By relying on objective data and analysis, positive economics aims to provide a clear and accurate picture of economic reality, much like Adam Smith's foundational work in economics.

### Key Takeaways:

- Positive economics describes the world as it is.
- It answers questions about the current state of the economy.
- It focuses on objective facts and data.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

One limitation of positive economics is that it relies on historical data, which may not accurately predict future economic trends. Another limitation is that it assumes that economic agents are rational, which may not always be the case in real-world scenarios. Additionally, positive economics may not account for external factors such as political or social influences that can impact economic outcomes. For instance, during times of crisis, economic agents may behave irrationally, leading to deviations from predicted outcomes. Furthermore, positive economics may struggle to capture complex interactions between different economic variables, potentially leading to oversimplification of economic phenomena.
