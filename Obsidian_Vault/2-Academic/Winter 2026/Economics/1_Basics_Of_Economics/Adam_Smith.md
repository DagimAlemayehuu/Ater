## 1. Mental Model

Imagine a small village where people trade goods with each other. Over time, as trade increases, the village becomes more prosperous. Adam Smith's ideas are like understanding how this village grows wealthier. He looked at how people make things and trade them, and how that makes a country richer. Just like how a farmer's market can become more efficient as more people participate, Smith saw that as more people trade and innovate, the whole economy benefits.

## 2. Causal Mechanism

Adam Smith, often regarded as the father of economics, published his seminal work 'An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of Wealth of Nations' in 1776. This work laid the groundwork for modern economics by exploring how nations accumulate wealth. Smith observed that when individuals act in their own self-interest, they can inadvertently benefit society as a whole through the creation of [[Economic_Systems]]. He argued that economic growth is driven by the division of labor and the efficiency gains from [[Capitalist_Economy]]. The concept of the 'invisible hand' suggests that individual pursuit of [[Human_Wants]] leads to socially beneficial outcomes, such as economic growth and [[Scarcity]] alleviation. Smith's theories have had a lasting impact on [[Microeconomics]] and continue to influence Positive Economics and Normative Economics today.

### Key Takeaways:

- Adam Smith is known as the father of economics
- He published 'An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of Wealth of Nations' in 1776
- His work laid the foundation for modern economics

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The theory assumes that individuals act rationally to maximize their wealth. However, in real-world scenarios, people may not always make rational decisions. For instance, during economic downturns, people might make impulsive financial decisions rather than rational ones. Additionally, the theory does not account for external factors such as government interventions or global events that can impact a nation's wealth. Furthermore, the concept of wealth and its causes may vary across different cultures and historical periods, which could limit the applicability of Smith's theories.
