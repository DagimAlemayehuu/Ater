## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you have a big chocolate cake and a yummy ice cream cone in front of you, and you can only choose one. If you choose to eat the ice cream cone, the opportunity cost is the chocolate cake you're giving up. You can't have both, so by picking the ice cream, you're using your "resource" (your stomach's space) for one treat, and that means you can't have the other treat. In economics, this is like allocating scarce resources - you have to make choices, and when you choose one thing, you're giving up the opportunity to have something else. Just like choosing between cake and ice cream, grown-ups have to make similar choices with money and resources, and that's why opportunity cost is important!

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of opportunity cost, a fundamental principle in microeconomics, has specific limitations and edge cases. One limitation arises when there are multiple alternatives, making it difficult to determine the most relevant opportunity cost. Additionally, opportunity cost assumes that resources are scarce and can be allocated efficiently, but in reality, resources may not always be fully utilized or may have indivisible characteristics, leading to complexities in calculating opportunity costs. Furthermore, when there are multiple goals or objectives, it becomes challenging to identify a single best alternative, and the concept of opportunity cost may not accurately capture the true costs of a decision. Moreover, opportunity cost also assumes rational decision-making, which may not always be the case, as individuals may make irrational choices or have limited information, further limiting the applicability of opportunity cost analysis.

## 5. Walkthrough

## Step 1: Define Scarcity and Resource Allocation

Given that economic resources are scarce, we must allocate them efficiently. For example, consider a farmer with 10 acres of land who can choose to grow either wheat or corn. If the farmer decides to use 10 acres for wheat, the land cannot be used for corn.

## Step 2: Identify the Tradeoff Using the Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)

The Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) illustrates the tradeoff between producing wheat and corn. Assume the farmer can produce 100 bushels of wheat or 200 bushels of corn with 10 acres. The PPF shows that to produce more wheat, less corn must be produced, and vice versa, due to the scarcity of land.

## 3: Determine the Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative that is given up. If the farmer chooses to produce 100 bushels of wheat, the opportunity cost is the 200 bushels of corn that could have been produced with the same land.

## 4: Calculate the Opportunity Cost

To calculate the opportunity cost, consider the tradeoff: 100 bushels of wheat = 200 bushels of corn. If the farmer decides to produce 50 bushels of wheat, they can produce 100 bushels of corn. The opportunity cost of producing 50 more bushels of wheat is 100 bushels of corn.

## 5: Apply the Concept to Decision Making

The concept of opportunity cost applies to decision-making units, such as consumers and producers. For instance, if a consumer chooses to spend $5 on a book, the opportunity cost is the other goods or services they could have purchased with that $5. Similarly, if a producer chooses to allocate resources to produce more of one good, the opportunity cost is the reduced production of another good.
