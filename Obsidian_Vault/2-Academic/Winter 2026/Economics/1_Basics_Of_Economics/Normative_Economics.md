## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a coffee shop, and you have to decide how to distribute the coffee among the customers. A normative economist would consider questions like 'What is the fairest way to distribute the coffee?' or 'How should the coffee be priced to ensure everyone can afford it?' This scenario illustrates how normative economics involves making value judgments about what ought to be, rather than simply describing how things are.

## 2. Foundational Concept

Normative economics is a branch of economics [[Branches_Of_Economics]] that focuses on what ought to be or what should be, based on one's value judgments about what is good or bad. It provides a framework for evaluating economic outcomes and making recommendations about how to improve them. In contrast to [[Positive_Economics]], which describes 'what is,' normative economics prescribes 'what should be.' This involves considering [[Scarcity]] and [[Human_Wants]], and evaluating how to allocate [[Economic_Resources]] in a way that is considered fair and efficient. By examining the Economic Systems and Economic Resources available, normative economists can provide insights into how to achieve Capitalist Economy goals and address Basic Economic Questions.

### Key Takeaways:

- Normative economics is concerned with what ought to be or what should be.
- It is based on value judgments about what is good or bad.
- Normative economics provides a framework for evaluating economic outcomes.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

One limitation of normative economics is that it relies heavily on subjective value judgments, which can vary greatly from person to person. For instance, what one person considers a good outcome, another might not. This subjectivity can make it challenging to reach a consensus on economic policies. Additionally, normative economics often involves making recommendations based on idealized scenarios, which may not accurately reflect real-world complexities. For example, it might assume that individuals act rationally, which is not always the case. Furthermore, normative economics can be influenced by cultural and social norms, which can change over time.
