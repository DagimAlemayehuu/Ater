# 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're a manager of a lemonade stand. The number of cups of lemonade you can supply to customers depends on factors like the amount of sugar and lemons you have, the number of cups and spoons available, and the skill level of your team. If you discover a new, more efficient way to mix the lemonade, you can prepare more cups with the same amount of effort. This means that you can supply more lemonade at each price level, causing the supply curve to shift to the right.

# 2. Economic Theory

A [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]] occurs when there is a change in the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to supply at each price level, resulting in a shift of the entire supply curve. This concept is closely related to the Law Of Supply, which states that as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied also increases, [[Ceteris_Paribus]]. The underlying mechanism of a shift in the supply curve is based on changes in [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], such as changes in production costs, technology, and expectations. For instance, an improvement in [[Change_In_Technology]] can increase the efficiency of production, allowing suppliers to produce more at each price level, which shifts the supply curve to the right. Similarly, an increase in production costs can decrease the quantity supplied at each price level, shifting the supply curve to the left. The [[Market_Equilibrium]] is also affected by a shift in the supply curve, as it can lead to changes in the market price and quantity.

# 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of a [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]] assumes that [[Ceteris_Paribus]] holds, meaning that all other factors remain constant. However, in reality, this assumption often breaks down. For example, during periods of [[Surplus_And_Shortage]], the supply curve may not shift smoothly, as suppliers may adjust their production levels in response to changes in inventory levels. Additionally, the [[Theory_Of_Demand]] can influence the supply curve, as changes in consumer preferences or income can affect the quantity demanded, which in turn affects the quantity supplied. Furthermore, the concept of [[Elasticity_Of_Supply]] is also important, as it determines how responsive suppliers are to changes in price. In cases where suppliers have a high degree of [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], a shift in the supply curve may lead to large changes in quantity supplied, while in cases where suppliers have a low degree of elasticity, the shift in the supply curve may lead to smaller changes in quantity supplied.

## 5. Walkthrough

Here's a 5-step technical walkthrough of how the concept of Shift In Supply Curve operates in International Trade Analysis:

1. **Initial State**: Suppose we have a supply curve for a particular good, say, wheat, with an initial quantity supplied of 100 units at a price of $10 per unit.
2. **Increase in Technology**: A new, more efficient farming technique is introduced, allowing farmers to increase their productivity by 20%. This leads to a decrease in production costs, from $5 to $4 per unit.
3. **Shift in Supply Curve**: With the new technology, farmers are willing to supply more wheat at each price level. The supply curve shifts to the right, indicating an increase in quantity supplied. For example, at a price of $10 per unit, the quantity supplied increases to 120 units.
4. **New Equilibrium**: As the supply curve shifts to the right, the market equilibrium changes. Assuming the demand curve remains constant, the new equilibrium price decreases to $9 per unit, and the quantity traded increases to 110 units.
5. **Outcome**: The shift in the supply curve leads to a more efficient market outcome, with lower prices and increased quantity traded. This benefits consumers, who can now purchase wheat at a lower price, and producers, who can sell more wheat at a slightly lower price but with reduced production costs.
