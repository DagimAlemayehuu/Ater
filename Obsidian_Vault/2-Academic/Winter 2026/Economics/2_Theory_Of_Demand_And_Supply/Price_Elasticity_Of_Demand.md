## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're obsessed with buying super cool, limited-edition sneakers that everyone wants. When the price is really high, say $100, you still buy a pair because you really want them, but your friend doesn't. That's 1 pair sold. Now, if the price suddenly drops to $50, you and your 9 friends rush to buy a pair each! That's 10 pairs sold. The price decreased by 50% and the number of pairs sold increased by 900%! This means people are very sensitive to price changes, and that's like having an elastic rubber band - it stretches a lot. But if the sneakers were really, really rare and the price dropped from $100 to $90, you and your friend might still only buy one pair each, because you really can't get more. The price decreased by 10% and the number of pairs sold only increased by 100%. That's like a stiff rubber band - it doesn't stretch much. That's basically what 'Price Elasticity Of Demand' measures: how much the quantity of something people want changes when its price changes.

## 2. Micro Theory

The concept of Price Elasticity Of Demand (PED) is a fundamental principle in microeconomics that measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its price, while holding all other influencing factors constant, as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption. The PED is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded in response to a 1% change in price, and is expressed as: PED = % ∆Q / % ∆P.

The PED is a dimensionless value that can be used to classify goods into different categories based on their elasticity. A good is said to be elastic if its PED is greater than 1, meaning that a small price change leads to a large change in quantity demanded. Conversely, a good is said to be inelastic if its PED is less than 1, indicating that a large price change leads to a small change in quantity demanded.

The PED is closely related to the [[Demand_Schedule]] and [[Demand_Curve]], which graphically represent the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. The PED can be derived from the [[Demand_Function]], which expresses the quantity demanded as a function of the price of the good, as well as other factors such as consumer income, prices of related goods, and consumer preferences.

The [[Theory_Of_Demand]] provides a framework for understanding the behavior of consumers in response to changes in market conditions, including changes in price. The [[Law_Of_Demand]] states that, ceteris paribus, an increase in the price of a good leads to a decrease in the quantity demanded, and vice versa. The PED provides a quantitative measure of this relationship.

The PED is influenced by several factors, including the availability of [[Substitutes_And_Complements]], the [[Taste_And_Preference]] of consumers, [[Consumer_Expectations]], [[Number_Of_Buyers]], and the [[Determinants_Of_Demand]]. For example, a good with close substitutes is likely to have a higher PED, as consumers can easily switch to alternative products in response to a price change.

The PED is also related to the [[Market_Demand]] and [[Market_Demand_Curve]], which represent the aggregate demand of all consumers in a market. The PED can be used to analyze the impact of changes in market conditions, such as a [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]], on the market equilibrium.

Furthermore, the PED has implications for [[Market_Equilibrium]], [[Surplus_And_Shortage]], and the [[Effects_Of_Shift_In_Demand_And_Supply]]. For instance, if the PED is high, a shift in the supply curve may lead to a large change in the market price, whereas if the PED is low, the same shift may lead to a smaller price change.

In contrast to [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], which measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in consumer income, the PED focuses specifically on the response to price changes. Understanding the PED is essential for businesses and policymakers to make informed decisions about pricing and output.

The PED formula, PED = % ∆Q / % ∆P, provides a useful tool for analyzing the price sensitivity of demand. By comparing the PED values of different goods and services, economists can gain insights into the [[Elasticity_Of_Supply]] and [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Supply_Formula]], which measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to changes in price.

In conclusion, the Price Elasticity Of Demand is a crucial concept in microeconomics that provides a quantitative measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in price. Its technical definition and mechanism are based on the PED formula, which is influenced by various factors, including the [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]] and [[Change_In_Demand]]. Understanding the PED is essential for businesses, policymakers, and economists to analyze market behavior and make informed decisions.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The price elasticity of demand (PED) measure has several limitations, particularly in its calculation and interpretation. For instance, PED assumes a linear demand curve, which often does not accurately represent real-world market behavior, and the elasticity value can vary along a non-linear demand curve. Additionally, PED calculations are sensitive to the direction of price and quantity changes, potentially yielding different elasticity values for price increases versus decreases, a phenomenon known as the 'directional effect'. Moreover, PED is also influenced by the time period considered, with demand often being more elastic over longer time horizons as consumers have more opportunity to adjust their behavior. Furthermore, PED does not account for changes in consumer income or prices of related goods, which can also impact demand and elasticity. Lastly, PED can be problematic to apply in situations where goods are not easily substitutable or when there are significant externalities, rendering the measure less informative for policy or business decisions.

## 5: Interpret the PED Value

The PED value of -1 indicates that the good has unitary elasticity. This means that for every 1% change in price, there is a 1% change in quantity demanded, but in the opposite direction (as indicated by the negative sign, which shows the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded). A PED of -1 signifies that the percentage change in quantity demanded is equal to the percentage change in price, categorizing the good as having unitary elastic demand.
