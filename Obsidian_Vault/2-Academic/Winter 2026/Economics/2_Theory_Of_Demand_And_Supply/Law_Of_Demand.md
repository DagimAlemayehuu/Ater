## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a lemonade stand on a hot summer day. The owner, let's call her Emma, is selling lemonade for 50 cents a cup. You and your friends really want lemonade, so you all buy a cup each. But then, Emma puts up a new sign that says "Lemonade: 1 dollar a cup!" Suddenly, you and your friends think, "Hmm, that's too expensive!" and you decide to buy only half a cup or even go to another stand that still sells lemonade for 50 cents. That's kind of like what happens with the Law of Demand. When the price (in this case, 50 cents to 1 dollar) goes up, people want to buy less of it (you and your friends buying fewer cups). And if Emma lowers the price back to 25 cents, you'll probably want to buy even more lemonade! So, the Law of Demand says that when the price of something goes up, people will want to buy less of it, and when the price goes down, people will want to buy more.

## 2. Micro Theory

The **Law of Demand** is a fundamental concept in microeconomics that describes the inverse relationship between the price of a product and the quantity demanded, ceteris paribus [[Ceteris_Paribus]]. This concept is rooted in the [[Theory_Of_Demand]], which examines how consumers make decisions about the goods and services they purchase.

Formally, the Law of Demand can be expressed through a [[Demand_Function]], which represents the relationship between the quantity demanded of a product and its price, while holding all other factors constant. The demand function can be written as: Qd = f(P), where Qd is the quantity demanded and P is the price of the product. The Law of Demand states that, as the price of the product increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa, assuming [[Ceteris_Paribus]].

This relationship is often graphically represented by a [[Demand_Curve]], which plots the quantity demanded against the price of the product. The demand curve typically slopes downward from left to right, indicating that as the price of the product increases, the quantity demanded decreases. The [[Demand_Schedule]], a table that shows the quantity demanded at different price levels, is another way to illustrate the Law of Demand.

The mechanism underlying the Law of Demand is based on the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], which measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in the price of the product. When the price of a product increases, consumers may seek [[Substitutes_And_Complements]] or reduce their consumption of the product, leading to a decrease in the quantity demanded.

The Law of Demand is influenced by various [[Determinants_Of_Demand]], including changes in [[Consumer_Expectations]], [[Number_Of_Buyers]], [[Taste_And_Preference]], and [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]]. For instance, if consumers expect the price of a product to increase in the future, they may increase their demand for it today. Similarly, an increase in the number of buyers or a change in consumer tastes and preferences can lead to an increase in demand.

The concept of [[Normal_And_Inferior_Goods]] also plays a role in understanding the Law of Demand. For normal goods, an increase in income leads to an increase in demand, while for inferior goods, an increase in income leads to a decrease in demand.

Changes in the [[Market_Demand]] and [[Market_Demand_Curve]] can occur due to shifts in the demand curve, which are caused by changes in the determinants of demand. A shift in the demand curve can lead to a change in the [[Market_Equilibrium]], resulting in a [[Surplus_And_Shortage]].

The interplay between the Law of Demand and the Law Of Supply helps determine the market equilibrium price and quantity. Understanding the Law of Demand and its underlying mechanisms is essential for analyzing the effects of changes in market conditions, such as a [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]], on the market equilibrium.

In conclusion, the Law of Demand provides a fundamental understanding of how consumers respond to changes in the price of a product, ceteris paribus. Its technical definition and mechanism are rooted in the [[Theory_Of_Demand]] and are influenced by various determinants of demand, ultimately shaping the [[Market_Demand_Curve]] and [[Market_Equilibrium]].

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The Law of Demand has several limitations and edge cases, including the existence of Veblen goods, where demand increases with price due to their prestige or status symbol connotation; Giffen goods, which are essential goods for which there are no close substitutes, leading to an increase in demand as their price rises; and the income effect being outweighed by the substitution effect in certain cases. Additionally, the law may not hold in situations where consumers are unaware of the price or have limited information about alternatives. Furthermore, the law assumes that the price change is not accompanied by a change in consumer income or tastes, and that there are no external factors influencing demand; if these assumptions are not met, the law may not hold. Lastly, in cases of panic buying or expectation of future price increases, demand may increase even if current price increases, thereby violating the law.
