The curve typically slopes downwards from left to right, illustrating that as the price of oranges decreases, the quantity demanded increases, and vice versa.

The [[Demand_Function]] and [[Demand_Curve]] are influenced by several factors, including [[Taste_And_Preference]], [[Number_Of_Buyers]], [[Consumer_Expectations]], and the prices of [[Substitutes_And_Complements]]. For instance, if consumer preferences shift in favor of oranges, the demand curve for oranges will shift to the right, indicating an increased quantity demanded at each price level.

The responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in price is measured by the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]]. This concept is crucial for understanding how changes in price affect the quantity demanded and for making pricing decisions.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The demand schedule, which outlines the quantity of a good demanded at various price points, has several limitations. For instance, assuming an individual household's demand for oranges per week, the demand schedule may not accurately capture the complexities of real-world consumer behavior, such as changes in consumer preferences, income, or prices of related goods, which can shift the demand curve. Additionally, the demand schedule typically assumes a linear relationship between price and quantity demanded, which may not hold in cases of non-linear demand or when consumers exhibit irrational behavior. Furthermore, the demand schedule may not account for edge cases such as rationing, where consumers are limited in the quantity they can purchase, or Giffen goods, where demand increases with price, defying the law of demand. These limitations highlight the need for nuanced analysis and consideration of real-world complexities when applying demand schedules to understand consumer behavior.
