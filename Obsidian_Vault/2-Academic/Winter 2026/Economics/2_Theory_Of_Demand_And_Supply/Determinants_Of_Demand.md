## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a lemonade stand on a sunny day. You really want lemonade, but how much you're willing to buy depends on several things. First, the price of lemonade (Px) - if it's super expensive, you might buy less. Your mom's pocket money (Y) today also matters - if she gives you more, you can buy more lemonade! You also think about other drinks like iced tea (Pr) - if it's really cheap and yummy, you might choose that instead. You even think about if lemonade will be cheaper tomorrow (Px,t+i) - if it will be, you might wait and buy less today. Your mom's future income (Yt+i) also affects your decision - if she gets a raise, she might give you more money to buy lemonade later. Additionally, if there are more people at the stand (N), you might want to buy more to share with friends. Lastly, if it's a really hot day (T), you'll want even more lemonade to quench your thirst! All these factors together decide how much lemonade you'll buy, just like they determine the demand for a product!

## 2. Micro Theory

The **Determinants of Demand** refer to the various factors that influence the quantity demanded of a good or service, denoted as Qx,t, which is a function of several variables. The demand function, Qx,t = f(Px,t, Yt, Pr,t, Px,t+i, Yt+i, N, T), provides a rigorous technical framework for analyzing these determinants.

The **Law of Demand**, a fundamental concept in microeconomics, states that, ceteris paribus (all other things being equal), an increase in the price of a good (Px,t) leads to a decrease in the quantity demanded. This is represented by the **Demand Curve**, a graphical representation of the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. The **Demand Schedule**, a tabular representation of this relationship, provides a more detailed analysis of the quantity demanded at various price levels.

The **Theory of Demand** posits that the quantity demanded of a good is influenced by several factors, including the price of the good (Px,t), consumer income (Yt), the prices of related goods (Pr,t), and consumer expectations about future prices (Px,t+i) and incomes (Yt+i). The **Market Demand** curve, which represents the total quantity demanded of a good by all consumers in a market, is a horizontal summation of individual demand curves.

The **Price Elasticity of Demand**, a measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in the price of a good, is an important concept in understanding the **Determinants of Demand**. The **Income Elasticity of Demand**, which measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in consumer income, is another crucial determinant.

The **Determinants of Demand** can be broadly categorized into several factors, including the price of the good (Px,t), consumer income (Yt), the prices of related goods (Pr,t), such as **Substitutes and Complements**, consumer expectations about future prices (Px,t+i) and incomes (Yt+i), the number of buyers (N), and **Taste and Preference** (T).

An increase in consumer income (Yt) can lead to an increase in the quantity demanded of a **Normal Good**, while a decrease in income can lead to a decrease in the quantity demanded. Conversely, an increase in income can lead to a decrease in the quantity demanded of an **Inferior Good**.

**Consumer Expectations** about future prices and incomes can also influence the quantity demanded. For instance, if consumers expect the price of a good to increase in the future, they may increase their current demand. Similarly, if consumers expect their incomes to increase in the future, they may increase their current demand for certain goods.

The **Number of Buyers** (N) in a market can also influence the quantity demanded. An increase in the number of buyers can lead to an increase in the market demand for a good.

**Taste and Preference** (T) play a crucial role in determining the quantity demanded of a good. A change in consumer tastes and preferences can lead to an increase or decrease in the quantity demanded of a good.

A **Change in Technology** can also influence the quantity demanded of a good. For instance, the development of new production technologies can lead to an increase in the supply of a good, which can, in turn, lead to a decrease in the price and an increase in the quantity demanded.

The **Market Demand Curve** can shift due to changes in any of the **Determinants of Demand**. A shift in the **Market Demand Curve** can lead to a change in the **Market Equilibrium**, resulting in a change in the price and quantity of the good.

The **Effects of Shift in Demand and Supply** can be analyzed using the **Demand and Supply Curve**. A shift in the demand curve can lead to a change in the market equilibrium, resulting in a surplus or shortage.

In conclusion, the **Determinants of Demand** are a set of factors that influence the quantity demanded of a good or service. Understanding these determinants is crucial in analyzing the behavior of consumers and the market demand for a good. The **Demand Function**, Qx,t = f(Px,t, Yt, Pr,t, Px,t+i, Yt+i, N, T), provides a rigorous technical framework for analyzing these determinants and their impact on the quantity demanded.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The determinants of demand, as represented by the demand function Qx,t = f(Px,t, Yt, Pr,t, Px,t+i, Yt+i, N, T), have several limitations and edge cases. For instance, the function assumes a static relationship between demand and its determinants, neglecting potential dynamic interactions and feedback loops, such as the impact of changes in current demand on future income (Yt+i) or the effect of expected price changes (Px,t+i) on current demand. Additionally, the function does not account for irrational consumer behavior, social influences, or external shocks, which can significantly influence demand. Furthermore, the function assumes that consumers have perfect information about prices (Px,t and Pr,t) and future market conditions, which is often not the case. The function also neglects the role of inventory stocks, lumpy purchases, and supply-side constraints, which can affect demand. Lastly, the function does not consider the possibility of non-linear relationships between demand and its determinants, or the presence of threshold effects, where changes in determinants only affect demand once a certain threshold is reached.

## 5. Walkthrough

## Step 1: Define the Demand Function

The demand function is given as Qx,t = f(Px,t, Yt, Pr,t, Px,t+i, Yt+i, N, T), where Qx,t represents the quantity demanded of a good or service at time t.

## Step 2: Identify the Determinants of Demand

The determinants of demand, as shown in the demand function, are:
- Px,t: the price of the good at time t
- Yt: consumer income at time t
- Pr,t: the price of related goods at time t
- Px,t+i: the expected price of the good at time t+i
- Yt+i: the expected consumer income at time t+i
- N: the number of consumers
- T: tastes and preferences

## 3: Apply the Law of Demand

According to the Law of Demand, ceteris paribus, an increase in the price of a good (Px,t) leads to a decrease in the quantity demanded (Qx,t). For example, if the price of a good increases from $5 to $6, the quantity demanded decreases.

## 4: Analyze the Demand Schedule

A demand schedule provides a detailed analysis of the quantity demanded at various price levels. For instance, if at a price of $5, the quantity demanded is 100 units, and at a price of $6, the quantity demanded is 80 units.

## 5: Graphical Representation - Demand Curve

The demand curve graphically represents the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. It typically slopes downward, indicating that as the price increases, the quantity demanded decreases. Using the previous example, if we plot the price of $5 against a quantity demanded of 100 units, and the price of $6 against a quantity demanded of 80 units, we get two points on the demand curve.
