# 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're a coffee lover who can either buy a latte from a coffee shop down the street or a similar coffee drink from a new café that just opened up across town. If the coffee shop down the street raises its prices, you might decide to buy your coffee from the new café instead. In this scenario, the latte from the coffee shop and the coffee drink from the new café are like two interchangeable toys - they serve the same purpose (getting your coffee fix), and you can easily switch between them. The mechanical components that map to the concept of substitute goods are: (1) the two coffee drinks (or goods) that satisfy the same desire (getting a coffee fix), and (2) the ease of switching between them (substitution).

# 2. Economic Theory

[[Substitutes_Goods]] are goods or services that can satisfy the same consumer desire or need, making them interchangeable in the eyes of the consumer. According to the [[Theory_Of_Demand]], the demand for a good is influenced by the availability and price of [[Substitutes_Goods]]. When the price of one good increases, the demand for its substitute good increases, as consumers switch to the cheaper alternative. This relationship is described by the [[Law_Of_Demand]], which states that as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, ceteris paribus ([[Ceteris_Paribus]]). The [[Demand_Schedule]] and [[Demand_Curve]] can be used to illustrate the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, including the impact of [[Substitutes_Goods]] on demand. The [[Demand_Function]] can also be used to model the relationship between the quantity demanded and various determinants of demand, including the price of [[Substitutes_Goods]].

# 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of [[Substitutes_Goods]] assumes that consumers have perfect information about the availability and prices of alternative goods, which is not always the case. Additionally, the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption underlying the [[Law_Of_Demand]] may not hold in situations where other factors, such as consumer preferences or income, change. The concept of [[Substitutes_Goods]] also breaks down in situations where goods are not perfect substitutes, such as when one good is of higher quality or has different features than the other. In such cases, the [[Demand_Curve]] may not shift as expected, and the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] may not accurately reflect the responsiveness of consumers to changes in price. Furthermore, the concept of [[Substitutes_Goods]] is closely related to [[Complementary_Goods]], as changes in the price of one good can affect the demand for complementary goods.

## 5. Walkthrough

Here's a 5-step technical walkthrough of how substitute goods operate in Market Strategy:

1. **Initial State**: A consumer has a demand for coffee, and there are two substitutes available: Latte (Good A) and Coffee Drink (Good B). Initially, the consumer buys 5 Lattes per week at $4 each.

2. **Price Increase**: The coffee shop that sells Lattes increases its price to $5 per Latte. This change makes the Latte more expensive compared to the Coffee Drink, which remains at $4.

3. **Switching to Substitute**: As a result of the price increase, the consumer switches to buying Coffee Drinks instead of Lattes. The demand for Coffee Drinks increases from 0 to 5 per week.

4. **Market Response**: The increased demand for Coffee Drinks leads to a shortage, prompting the café that sells Coffee Drinks to adjust its price to $4.50. The coffee shop selling Lattes notices a decrease in sales and considers lowering its price back to $4 to compete.

5. **Equilibrium Adjustment**: Over time, the market adjusts as consumers and producers respond to price changes. The consumer may now buy a mix of both Lattes and Coffee Drinks based on their new prices, and the suppliers adjust their production levels. The prices of both goods may stabilize at a point where the demand for each equals its supply, reflecting the substitution effect.
