# 1. Mental Model

Imagine you have a lemonade stand, and you usually make 10 cups of lemonade every day. One day, your friend gives you a super cool machine that makes lemonade really fast, so now you can make 20 cups of lemonade in the same amount of time. This means you can supply more lemonade without changing the price, which is like shifting your supply to the right.

# 2. Economic Theory

A [[Shifts_In_Supply_Curve]] occurs when the supply of a good or service changes in response to a change in one of its determinants, such as [[Change_In_Technology]], leading to a shift of the entire [[Supply_Schedule]] and [[Supply_Curve]] to the left or right, assuming [[Ceteris_Paribus]]. This shift represents a change in the quantity supplied at each price level. For example, an improvement in technology can cause a shift to the right, while a decrease in the number of suppliers can cause a shift to the left.

## 4. Walkthrough

* The initial supply curve (A) represents the relationship between the price of lemonade and the quantity supplied.
* An increase in technology (e.g., the lemonade machine) causes a rightward shift (B) of the supply curve to a new position (C).
* At the new supply curve (C), the quantity supplied increases (D) because the machine allows for more efficient production.
* As a result, the market price (E) tends to decrease due to the increased supply.

## 5. Market Failures

This concept may fail when there are external factors that affect supply, such as government regulations or changes in input prices. Additionally, if the increase in technology is not evenly distributed among suppliers, some firms may not experience a rightward shift in their supply curve. Edge cases to watch out for include changes in consumer preferences or unexpected changes in weather conditions.
