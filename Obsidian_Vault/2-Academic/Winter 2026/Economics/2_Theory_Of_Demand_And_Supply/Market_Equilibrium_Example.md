## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a school bake sale. You have 10 cupcakes to sell at a price you decide, let's say 50 cents each. If you price them too high, like $1 each, only 2 kids might buy them, and you'll have 8 cupcakes left over. But if you price them too low, like 25 cents each, 15 kids might want to buy them, but you only have 10. The magic happens when you price them at 40 cents each, and exactly 10 kids want to buy them - that's market equilibrium! You're selling all your cupcakes, and the kids are happy with the price. The price is just right, so you don't have any leftovers, and the kids aren't complaining about the cost. That's when the supply (your cupcakes) meets the demand (the kids' desire for cupcakes), and it's called market equilibrium.

## 2. Micro Theory

In microeconomics, market equilibrium is a state where the quantity of a good or service that suppliers are willing to sell (supply) equals the quantity that buyers are willing to buy (demand). A market equilibrium example illustrates this concept. To rigorously define market equilibrium, we must begin with the [[Demand_Function]], which represents the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded by consumers. The demand function is typically expressed as Qd = f(P), where Qd is the quantity demanded and P is the price.

The [[Law_Of_Demand]] states that, ceteris paribus (all else being equal, as per [[Ceteris_Paribus]]), as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa. This relationship is often depicted graphically as a [[Demand_Curve]], which is a downward-sloping curve showing how the quantity demanded changes with price. The [[Demand_Schedule]], a table listing the quantity demanded at various price points, underlies this curve.

On the supply side, the Market Supply curve represents the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that suppliers are willing to sell. The supply function is expressed as Qs = g(P), where Qs is the quantity supplied. The Law Of Supply implies that, ceteris paribus, as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied also increases. This relationship is depicted as an upward-sloping Supply Curve.

Market equilibrium occurs at the point where the [[Market_Demand_Curve]] and the Market Supply Curve intersect. At this equilibrium point, the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. The price at which this occurs is known as the equilibrium price (Pe), and the corresponding quantity is the equilibrium quantity (Qe).

To illustrate a market equilibrium example, consider a simple market for a particular good. Assume the [[Demand_Function]] is Qd = 100 - P and the Supply Function is Qs = P - 20. Setting Qd = Qs to find the equilibrium, we get 100 - P = P - 20. Solving for P gives 2P = 120, and thus P = 60. Substituting P = 60 into either the demand or supply function yields Qe = 40.

The concept of market equilibrium is crucial in understanding how markets allocate resources. Changes in [[Determinants_Of_Demand]], such as [[Taste_And_Preference]], [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], [[Number_Of_Buyers]], and [[Consumer_Expectations]], can shift the [[Market_Demand_Curve]]. Similarly, changes in [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], such as [[Change_In_Technology]] and [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]], can shift the Market Supply Curve. These shifts can lead to a new market equilibrium, resulting in changes in the equilibrium price and quantity.

The stability of market equilibrium can be affected by the [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] and Price Elasticity Of Supply. For instance, if demand is highly elastic, a small price change can lead to a large change in quantity demanded. The [[Surplus_And_Shortage]] that may occur due to shifts in demand and supply can also influence the speed at which a new equilibrium is reached.

In conclusion, a market equilibrium example provides a practical illustration of how the forces of demand and supply interact to determine the price and quantity of a good in a market economy. Understanding the technical definitions and mechanisms underlying market equilibrium, including the roles of [[Market_Demand]], [[Market_Demand_Curve]], and [[Market_Equilibrium]], is essential for analyzing the behavior of markets and the effects of external influences.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The market equilibrium example assumes a perfectly competitive market with numerous buyers and sellers, which may not hold in real-world scenarios where firms have varying degrees of market power. Additionally, it relies on the ceteris paribus assumption, which overlooks external factors such as changes in consumer preferences, technological advancements, or government interventions that can shift demand and supply curves. Furthermore, the example often simplifies the relationship between price and quantity demanded/supplied, neglecting complexities such as non-linear demand curves, income effects, and substitution effects. Moreover, it typically assumes that market participants have perfect information, which is rarely the case in reality, and that transactions occur without any costs or frictions, which can lead to inefficiencies. Lastly, the example may not account for edge cases such as rationing, queuing, or black markets that can emerge when markets are not in equilibrium.

## 5. Walkthrough

## Step 1: Define the Demand Function and Demand Curve

The demand function is given as Qd = f(P), where Qd is the quantity demanded and P is the price. For simplicity, let's assume a linear demand function: Qd = a - bP, where 'a' and 'b' are constants. The demand curve is a graphical representation of this function, typically downward-sloping, illustrating that as the price (P) increases, the quantity demanded (Qd) decreases.

## Step 2: Define the Supply Function and Supply Curve

The supply function represents the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that suppliers are willing to sell. It is expressed as Qs = g(P), where Qs is the quantity supplied. According to the Law of Supply, as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied also increases, assuming all else is equal. A simple linear supply function could be Qs = c + dP, where 'c' and 'd' are constants. The supply curve is an upward-sloping curve showing how the quantity supplied changes with price.

## 3: Determine the Market Equilibrium Condition

Market equilibrium occurs where the quantity demanded (Qd) equals the quantity supplied (Qs). Therefore, to find the market equilibrium, we set Qd = Qs and solve for P. Using the linear functions from Steps 1 and 2: a - bP = c + dP.

## 4: Solve for Equilibrium Price (P) and Quantity (Q)

Rearrange the equation from Step 3 to solve for P: a - c = dP + bP, which simplifies to a - c = P(d + b). Solving for P gives: P = (a - c) / (d + b). Once the equilibrium price (P) is found, substitute it back into either the demand or supply function to find the equilibrium quantity (Q). For example, using the demand function: Q = a - b((a - c) / (d + b)).

## 5: Interpret the Market Equilibrium Example in Labor Market Economics

In the context of labor market economics, the good or service being analyzed is labor. The demand for labor comes from firms, which are willing to hire workers at a certain wage (price of labor), and the supply of labor comes from households, which are willing to work at that wage. The market equilibrium example illustrates how the wage (price) and quantity of labor (number of workers or hours worked) are determined in a competitive labor market. At the equilibrium wage, the number of workers that firms want to hire equals the number of workers willing to work.
