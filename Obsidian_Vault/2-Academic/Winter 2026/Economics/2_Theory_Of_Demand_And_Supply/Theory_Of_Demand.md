## 1. Mental Model

Imagine you're at a candy store and your favorite lollipops are on sale for $1 each. You really want 5 of them, but if the store owner suddenly says, "Oops, I'm feeling generous, I'll give you 5 lollipops for 50 cents each!" You'd probably want even more than 5, right? But now imagine the store owner says, "Actually, I'm not feeling so generous, 5 lollipops will cost you $5 each!" You'd probably want fewer than 5. That's kind of like the theory of demand. When the price of something (like lollipops) goes down, people want more of it, and when the price goes up, people want less of it. This is called the law of demand, and it helps us understand how people make choices about what to buy when prices change.

## 2. Micro Theory

The Theory Of Demand is a fundamental concept in microeconomics that examines the relationship between the quantity demanded of a good or service and its price, while holding all other influencing factors constant, as per the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption. This theory is built on the [[Law_Of_Demand]], which states that, ceteris paribus, as the price of a good or service increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa. 

The demand for a good or service can be represented by a [[Demand_Schedule]], which is a table that shows the quantity demanded at different price levels. When graphically illustrated, the demand schedule becomes a [[Demand_Curve]], typically downward sloping, indicating that as the price of the good or service decreases, the quantity demanded increases. 

Mathematically, the relationship between the quantity demanded and the factors influencing demand can be expressed using a [[Demand_Function]], which is a mathematical representation of the demand for a good or service. The demand function is usually expressed as Qd = f(P, I, T, Pc, Ps, E), where Qd is the quantity demanded, P is the price of the good, I is the consumer's income, T is the taste and preference of the consumer, Pc is the price of complements, Ps is the price of substitutes, and E is the consumer's expectations.

The [[Market_Demand]] refers to the total quantity demanded of a good or service by all consumers in a market, and it can be graphically represented by a [[Market_Demand_Curve]], which is the horizontal summation of individual demand curves. 

One of the key concepts in the Theory Of Demand is [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Demand]], which measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in the price of the good or service. The price elasticity of demand can be calculated using the formula: Ed = (dQd/Qd) / (dP/P). 

The [[Income_Elasticity_Of_Demand]] measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in consumers' income. The [[Determinants_Of_Demand]], such as changes in consumer taste and preferences, income, prices of related goods (substitutes and complements), and consumer expectations, can cause a [[Change_In_Demand]], resulting in a shift of the demand curve.

Goods can be classified into [[Normal_And_Inferior_Goods]] based on their response to changes in consumer income. [[Substitutes_And_Complements]] refer to goods that can be used in place of or together with the good in question. 

The [[Consumer_Expectations]] about future prices or income can influence current demand. An increase in the [[Number_Of_Buyers]] in a market can lead to an increase in market demand. A change in [[Taste_And_Preference]] of consumers can also influence demand. 

The interaction between demand and supply in a market determines the [[Market_Equilibrium]], which is the point at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. A [[Surplus_And_Shortage]] can occur if there is a mismatch between the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied. 

The [[Effects_Of_Shift_In_Demand_And_Supply]] on market equilibrium can be analyzed using the demand and supply framework. For instance, an increase in demand, ceteris paribus, leads to an increase in the equilibrium price and quantity. 

The theory of demand has numerous applications in business and policy-making, as understanding the factors influencing demand is crucial for making informed decisions. For example, in a [[Market_Equilibrium_Example]], if the demand for a good increases while the supply remains constant, the equilibrium price and quantity will increase. 

The analysis of demand is also closely related to the [[Change_In_Technology]] and [[Shift_In_Supply_Curve]], which can affect the supply of goods and services. The [[Elasticity_Of_Supply]] and [[Price_Elasticity_Of_Supply_Formula]] can help in understanding the responsiveness of suppliers to changes in price. 

The [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]] include factors such as the availability of inputs, technology, and the time period considered. Overall, the Theory Of Demand provides a comprehensive framework for analyzing the behavior of consumers in a market economy.

## 5. Walkthrough

**Step 1: Define the Demand Function**

The demand function is a mathematical representation of the relationship between the quantity demanded (Qd) of a good or service and its influencing factors. The general form of a demand function is:

Qd = f(P, I, T, Psub, Psup, A)

Where:
- Qd = quantity demanded
- P = price of the good or service
- I = consumer income
- T = consumer tastes and preferences
- Psub = price of substitute goods
- Psup = price of supplementary goods
- A = advertising and other marketing efforts

Assuming a linear demand function for simplicity:

Qd = a - bP + cI + dT + ePsub - fPsup + gA

**Step 2: Apply the Ceteris Paribus Assumption**

To analyze the relationship between the quantity demanded and the price of the good or service, we apply the ceteris paribus assumption. This means we hold all other influencing factors (I, T, Psub, Psup, A) constant. The demand function now focuses solely on the relationship between Qd and P:

Qd = a - bP

**Step 3: Derive the Demand Schedule**

Using the simplified demand function, we can derive a demand schedule by calculating the quantity demanded at different price levels, while keeping all other factors constant.

| Price (P) | Quantity Demanded (Qd) |
| --- | --- |
| $10 | 100 |
| $9  | 120 |
| $8  | 140 |
| $7  | 160 |
| $6  | 180 |

**Step 4: Graph the Demand Curve**

By plotting the data from the demand schedule, we can graph the demand curve. The demand curve is typically downward sloping, indicating that as the price of the good or service decreases, the quantity demanded increases.

**Step 5: Interpret the Law of Demand**

The graphed demand curve illustrates the Law of Demand, which states that, ceteris paribus, as the price of a good or service increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa. This is evident from the negative slope of the demand curve. For example, as the price decreases from $10 to $6, the quantity demanded increases from 100 to 180, demonstrating the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
