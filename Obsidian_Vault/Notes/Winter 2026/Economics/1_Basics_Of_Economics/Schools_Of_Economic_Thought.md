## 1. Mental Model

Imagine economics as a growing tree. At its roots, thinkers argued about whether economics was just about counting gold (Wealth). As the tree grew, they argued it was about human happiness (Welfare). Later, it became about the logic of survival with limited tools (Scarcity). Today, the branches focus on how the whole tree grows over time (Growth). Each school of thought is a different lens through which we view human activity.

## 2. Causal Mechanism

The definition and scope of economics have evolved through four major "schools" or perspectives, reflecting the changing priorities of society:

1.  **Wealth Definition (Adam Smith, 1776)**:
    - **Focus**: The production and consumption of wealth.
    - **Key Text**: "An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of Wealth of Nations".
    - **View**: Economics is a subject that studies how nations get rich.
2.  **Welfare Definition (Alfred Marshall)**:
    - **Focus**: Human welfare and "the ordinary business of life".
    - **View**: Economics is a study of mankind in the ordinary business of life; it examines that part of individual and social action which is most closely connected with the attainment and use of the material requisites of wellbeing.
3.  **Scarcity Definition (Lionel Robbins)**:
    - **Focus**: Choice under constraints.
    - **View**: Economics is the science which studies human behavior as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses. This is the foundation of modern microeconomics.
4.  **Growth Definition (Paul Samuelson)**:
    - **Focus**: Dynamic allocation and long-term expansion.
    - **View**: Economics studies how people and society choose, with or without the use of money, to employ scarce productive resources that could have alternative uses, to produce various commodities over time.

### Key Takeaways:
- **Adam Smith** shifted economics into a distinct academic subject.
- The **Scarcity** definition is currently the most formal and widely accepted "neutral" definition.
- The **Growth** definition adds the dimension of *time* to the problem of allocation.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

- **No Single Definition**: Because economics is a social science, it evolves with human behavior. A definition that fits 18th-century industrialization (Wealth) may not fully capture 21st-century environmental concerns.
- **Overlapping Goals**: Modern economics often combines elements of all four (e.g., using Wealth to achieve Welfare within a framework of Scarcity).

## 4. Comparison Table

| School | Key Proponent | Primary Focus | Definition Core |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Wealth** | Adam Smith | Accumulation | "Science of Wealth" |
| **Welfare** | Alfred Marshall | Material Wellbeing | "Ordinary business of life" |
| **Scarcity** | Lionel Robbins | Choice/Constraints | "Ends and scarce means" |
| **Growth** | Paul Samuelson | Time/Development | "Allocation over time" |

## 5. Walkthrough

**Step 1:** Early economists focus on how to make the state rich (Wealth).
**Step 2:** Thinkers realize wealth is only useful if it improves life (Welfare).
**Step 3:** The realization hits that resources are finite, forcing hard choices regardless of total wealth (Scarcity).
**Step 4:** Modern economists look at how to expand those resources and improve allocation technology (Growth).
