## 1. Mental Model

In a small, family-owned textile workshop, skilled artisans weave intricate patterns on traditional looms, a process that requires great attention to detail and manual dexterity. This setting illustrates a labour-intensive technique, where the production process relies heavily on human labour and skill rather than machines. The workshop's output is limited by the number of skilled workers available, and production costs are largely driven by labour costs. As demand for their products increases, the workshop must carefully consider how to scale up production without compromising quality.

## 2. Foundational Concept

Labour-intensive techniques are characterised by a high ratio of labour to capital, meaning that production relies heavily on human effort rather than machines or technology. In contrast to [[Capital_Intensive_Techniques]], which heavily rely on machinery and automation, labour-intensive techniques often require a larger workforce to achieve the same level of output. The textile workshop example highlights the importance of skilled labour in such techniques. Economists studying [[Economic_Growth]] and How To Produce must consider the implications of labour-intensive techniques on [[Limited_Resources]] and [[Scarcity]].

### Key Takeaways:

- The car industry and the computer industry, mentioned in the source, likely employ different techniques, with the car industry possibly using more capital-intensive methods and the computer industry relying on highly skilled labour.
- A nuance of labour-intensive techniques is that they often require a high level of skill and training, which can limit their scalability.
- Understanding labour-intensive techniques matters for the broader course because it helps address Basic Economic Questions, such as how to allocate Limited Resources effectively.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

While labour-intensive techniques can be effective for producing high-quality, customised products, they may not be suitable for large-scale production or industries with high demand. Additionally, these techniques can be vulnerable to disruptions in the labour market, such as changes in workforce availability or increases in labour costs. In some cases, labour-intensive techniques may also be associated with [[Market_Failure]] if they lead to worker exploitation or neglect of worker safety. The assumption that labour is readily available and can be easily substituted for capital may not always hold, particularly in industries with specialised skill requirements.
