## 1. Mental Model

The government of a small country is debating whether to implement a policy of free trade or protectionism. Some argue that free trade will benefit the economy, while others claim that it will lead to job losses. This debate illustrates the role of personal opinions and values in economic decision-making. Economists may analyze the data, but ultimately, a value judgment must be made.

## 2. Foundational Concept

Normative economics involves making value judgments or expressing opinions about what the economy should be like. This approach is distinct from [[Positive_Economics]], which focuses on objective facts and data. The science of economics, which began to take shape with Adam Smith's book around two hundred years ago, often deals with [[Basic_Economic_Questions]] that require both positive and normative analysis. For instance, when evaluating [[Economic_Growth]], economists may use [[Deductive_Reasoning]] and [[Inductive_Reasoning]] to understand the implications of different policies.

### Key Takeaways:

- Economics as a distinct subject started with Adam Smith's book about two hundred years ago.
- Normative economics is concerned with expressing opinions or value judgments about the economy.
- Understanding Scarcity, Unlimited Wants, and Limited Resources is crucial for making informed decisions in normative economics.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

Normative economics can be subjective and influenced by personal biases, making it challenging to reach a consensus on economic policies. Additionally, different [[Economic_Systems]] may prioritize various goals, such as efficiency, equality, or environmental sustainability, leading to conflicting normative judgments. Furthermore, [[Market_Failure]] and other real-world complications can limit the effectiveness of normative economic policies.
