## 1. Mental Model
Imagine a small island economy that can produce either fish or coconuts. The islanders must decide how many fish and coconuts to produce given their available resources, such as fishing nets and coconut trees. The production possibilities frontier would show the various combinations of fish and coconuts that the islanders can produce. For example, they could produce a lot of fish and few coconuts, or a lot of coconuts and few fish. The islanders must choose the combination that best meets their needs and wants.

## 2. Foundational Concept
The concept of [[What_To_Produce]] is a fundamental question in economics, as economies cannot produce everything and must therefore decide what goods and services to produce and in what quantities. The [[Production_Possibilities_Frontier]] is a graphical representation of the various combinations of output that an economy can produce given its available factors of production and production technology. In the context of the SOURCE CONTEXT, there are two broad classes of output to be produced over the year. The core facts are: (1) economies face [[Scarcity]], (2) the Production Possibilities Frontier shows the possible combinations of output, and (3) there are two broad classes of output. These facts imply that economies must make choices about what to produce, and the Production Possibilities Frontier is a tool used to analyze these choices. The concept of What To Produce is closely related to Basic Economic Questions and Microeconomics.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases
The production possibilities frontier assumes that the economy has full employment of resources and that technology is fixed. However, in reality, these assumptions may not always hold. For instance, during economic downturns, resources may not be fully utilized, and technological advancements can change the production possibilities over time. Additionally, the model does not account for the distribution of income and wealth, which can affect the types of goods and services produced. The model also assumes that the economy produces only two types of goods, which is a simplification of real-world economies that produce a vast array of goods and services.
