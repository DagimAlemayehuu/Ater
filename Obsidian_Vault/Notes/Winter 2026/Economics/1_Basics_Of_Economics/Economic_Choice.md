## 1. Mental Model
Imagine a simple coffee shop where customers have to choose between different types of coffee. The shop has limited quantities of each type, and customers have to decide how to allocate their scarce resources (money and time) to make a choice. This scenario illustrates the concept of economic choice, where individuals have to make decisions based on their preferences and the limited availability of resources.

## 2. Foundational Concept
| The concept of economic choice is rooted in the definition of [[Definition_Of_Economics]], which is the study of how human beings make choices to use [[Scarcity|scarce resources]]. The core fact is that humans have unlimited [[Human_Wants]] but face limited resources, leading to the need for choice. The theory of economic choice involves understanding how individuals make decisions to allocate their resources, given the constraints of scarcity and the need to satisfy their wants. This concept is central to [[Microeconomics]], which studies the behavior of individual economic units, such as households and firms, and how they make choices about how to allocate their resources. The theory of economic choice also relies on the concept of [[Positive_Economics]], which focuses on the description and analysis of economic phenomena, rather than Normative Economics, which involves value judgments and prescriptions for economic policy. |

### Key Takeaways:
- Economics is the study of how human beings make choices to use scarce resources.
- The definition of economics is not universally accepted.
- Economics involves making choices due to Scarcity.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases
The concept of economic choice is limited by the assumption that humans make rational decisions. However, in real-world scenarios, humans may not always act rationally due to factors such as emotions, cognitive biases, or limited information. Additionally, the concept of scarcity implies that there are always trade-offs involved in making choices, but it does not account for situations where resources may be abundant or freely available. Furthermore, economic choice assumes that individuals have the freedom to make choices, but in reality, choices may be constrained by factors such as social norms, laws, or institutional barriers.
