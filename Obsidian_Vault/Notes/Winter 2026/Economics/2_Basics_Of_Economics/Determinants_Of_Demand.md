## 1. Mental Model

At a local coffee shop, the owner notices that when the price of a latte increases, the number of lattes sold decreases. Conversely, when the price drops, more lattes are sold. This observation aligns with the basic principle that the higher the price of a product, all else equal, the less of it will be demanded. The owner must consider these dynamics when setting prices.

## 2. Foundational Concept

The theory of demand describes the relationship between consumer demand for goods and services and their prices in the market. A fundamental aspect of this relationship is that, all else equal, the higher the price of a product, the less of it will be demanded, and vice versa. This concept relies heavily on the 'Ceteris paribus' assumption, which means that other things remain equal or unchanged. For instance, when analyzing the demand for a product, factors such as [[Technological_Advancement]], [[Market_Demand]], and changes in consumer preferences are held constant. The [[Demand_Schedule]] and [[Demand_Curve]] are tools used to visualize and represent how the quantity demanded of a product changes in response to price changes, assuming [[Ceteris_Paribus]].

### Key Takeaways:

- The higher the price of a product, all else equal, the less of it will be demanded.
- The concept of demand is closely related to the Demand Function, which expresses the relationship between the quantity demanded of a product and its price.
- Understanding the determinants of demand is crucial for businesses to set optimal prices and for policymakers to assess the impact of taxes or subsidies on market outcomes.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The theory of demand assumes that consumers have perfect information about the market and that their preferences remain constant. However, in reality, consumers' preferences and perceptions can change over time due to various factors, including marketing and [[Substitute_Goods]]. Additionally, the [[Ceteris_Paribus]] assumption can be limiting, as it does not account for changes in income, tastes, or prices of related goods, which can significantly impact demand. Therefore, while the theory provides a foundational understanding, real-world applications require consideration of these complexities.

## 4. Determinants of Demand

### Determinants of Demand

The demand for a product is influenced by several factors. These determinants can be categorized into two main groups: 
1. **Price of the Product**: The most direct determinant. Generally, as the price of a product increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa.

2. **Non-Price Factors**:
    - **Income of Consumers**: An increase in consumer income typically increases demand for normal goods, while demand for inferior goods decreases.
    - **Tastes and Preferences**: A favorable change in tastes and preferences increases demand, while an unfavorable change decreases it.
    - **Prices of Related Goods**: 
        - **Substitutes**: A decrease in the price of a substitute good decreases demand for the original product, and vice versa.
        - **Complements**: A decrease in the price of a complementary good increases demand for the original product, and vice versa.
    - **Population and Demographics**: An increase in population or a demographic shift can increase or decrease demand for certain products.
    - **Seasonality**: Demand for some products varies with seasons.
    - **Technological Changes**: Can increase or decrease demand depending on how they affect the product's utility or availability.

### Example: Coffee Shop Scenario

Consider a local coffee shop. The demand for lattes can be influenced by:

- **Price of Lattes**: If the coffee shop increases the price of a latte, customers may buy fewer lattes.
- **Consumer Income**: If the local population experiences an economic boom, they might buy more lattes.
- **Tastes and Preferences**: If a health trend encourages people to consume less caffeine, demand for lattes might decrease.
- **Substitutes**: If a new tea shop opens nearby, offering a substitute beverage, demand for lattes might decrease.

Understanding these determinants helps businesses and policymakers make informed decisions about pricing, production, and market interventions.
