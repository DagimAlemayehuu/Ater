## 1. Mental Model

At a local farmer's market, the quantity of fresh strawberries that consumers are willing to buy equals the quantity that farmers are willing to sell, resulting in an equilibrium quantity. This balance is achieved when the demand and supply curves intersect. For instance, if consumers demand 100 baskets of strawberries and farmers are willing to supply 100 baskets, the market reaches equilibrium. This equilibrium quantity is the point at which the quantity demanded $Q_d$ equals the quantity supplied $Q_s$.

## 2. Foundational Concept

The equilibrium quantity is the quantity of a good or service that is bought and sold in a market when the quantity demanded $Q_d$ equals the quantity supplied $Q_s$. This concept is central to [[Market_Equilibrium]], where the demand and supply curves intersect. According to the source, at this point, $Q_d = Q_s$. The equilibrium quantity is influenced by various factors, including [[Technological_Advancement]], which can increase the supply of a good, and [[Determinants_Of_Elasticity_Of_Supply]], which affect the responsiveness of suppliers to changes in market conditions. Understanding equilibrium quantity is essential for analyzing [[Market_Demand]] and [[Law_Of_Supply]].

### Key Takeaways:

- A technological advancement in farming can increase the supply of strawberries, potentially changing the equilibrium quantity.
- The ease of entering the market for farmers can affect the elasticity of supply and thus influence the equilibrium quantity.
- Understanding equilibrium quantity helps in analyzing how changes in demand or supply affect market prices and quantities.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of equilibrium quantity assumes that markets are perfectly competitive and that there are no externalities. In reality, markets may not always reach equilibrium, especially in cases of [[Time_And_Eligibility]] constraints or when there are [[Substitutes_And_Eligibility]] that affect consumer choices. Additionally, the presence of [[Inferior_Goods]] or [[Normal_Goods]] can complicate the analysis of equilibrium quantity. The equilibrium quantity concept also relies on the assumption that suppliers and consumers have perfect information, which is rarely the case in real-world markets. Therefore, while the equilibrium quantity provides a useful theoretical framework, it may not accurately reflect the complexities of real-world markets.
