## 1. Mental Model

At a local farmer's market, the demand for fresh strawberries changes drastically when a new shipment arrives, causing prices to fluctuate. On a sunny summer day, more people are willing to buy strawberries, but when a rainstorm hits, demand drops. This scenario illustrates how demand can shift based on external factors. The market equilibrium is constantly adjusting to meet the changing demand.

## 2. Foundational Concept

Demand is a fundamental concept in economics that refers to the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price level. The demand for a product is typically represented by a [[Demand_Curve]], which shows the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded. The point at which the demand curve intersects with the supply curve is known as [[Market_Equilibrium]], where $Q_d = Q_s$. Changes in demand can be influenced by various factors, including changes in consumer preferences, income, and the availability of [[Substitute_Goods]].

### Key Takeaways:

- A change in technology can increase supply, but it does not directly affect demand, as seen in the discussion on [[Technological_Advancement]].
- The concept of demand is crucial in understanding [[Market_Demand]] and making informed decisions in business and policy-making.
- Demand is not static and can shift due to various factors, including changes in consumer preferences and income levels.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of demand assumes that consumers have perfect information about the market and make rational decisions, which is not always the case in reality. Additionally, demand can be influenced by external factors such as government policies, cultural trends, and environmental changes, which can be difficult to quantify. Furthermore, the demand for certain goods, such as [[Inferior_Goods]], may not follow the typical laws of demand, and may require special consideration.
