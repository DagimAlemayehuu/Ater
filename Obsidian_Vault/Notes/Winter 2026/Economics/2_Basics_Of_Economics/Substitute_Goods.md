## 1. Mental Model

In a small town, a coffee shop and a tea house are located across from each other. The coffee shop sells a cup of coffee for $2, while the tea house sells a cup of tea for $1.80. Customers who prefer coffee but find the price too high can easily switch to tea, and vice versa. This scenario illustrates how the presence of substitute goods, such as coffee and tea, affects consumer behavior and market dynamics.

## 2. Foundational Concept

The concept of substitute goods refers to products or services that can satisfy the same consumer needs or wants, making them interchangeable. Ceteris paribus, or assuming all other factors are constant, the availability of substitute goods allows consumers to switch between products in response to changes in price or quality. For instance, if the price of coffee increases, consumers may opt for tea instead, demonstrating the substitution effect. This concept is closely related to [[Demand_Schedule]], [[Demand_Function]], and [[Demand_Curve]], as changes in the price of substitute goods can shift demand curves. The presence of substitute goods also influences [[Market_Equilibrium]], as firms must consider the availability of alternatives when setting prices.

### Key Takeaways:

- The ceteris paribus assumption allows economists to isolate the effect of a single change, such as a price increase, on consumer behavior.
- Substitute goods, like coffee and tea, can be interchangeable, making their demand curves related.
- Understanding substitute goods is crucial for businesses, as it helps them anticipate consumer responses to price changes and adjust their strategies accordingly.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

The concept of substitute goods assumes that consumers have perfect information about available alternatives and can easily switch between them. However, in reality, consumers may face limitations, such as lack of knowledge about substitute goods or switching costs. Additionally, the availability of substitute goods can vary across regions or markets, affecting the concept's applicability. Furthermore, the ceteris paribus assumption may not always hold, as changes in consumer preferences or income can influence demand for substitute goods. The concept of substitute goods also relies on the [[Law_Of_Supply]] and [[Market_Demand]], which may not always be perfectly elastic.
