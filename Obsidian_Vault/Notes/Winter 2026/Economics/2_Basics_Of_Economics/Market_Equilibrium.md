## 1. Mental Model

At a local farmer's market, the demand for fresh strawberries equals the supply provided by local farmers. On a particular Saturday, the market reaches a point where the quantity of strawberries that consumers are willing to buy matches the quantity that farmers are willing to sell. This balance is achieved at a specific price, where the number of strawberry baskets demanded equals the number supplied. As a result, the market is in equilibrium.

## 2. Causal Mechanism

Market equilibrium occurs when market demand equals market supply, represented by the equation $Q_d = Q_s$. This concept is crucial in understanding how markets operate, as it determines the price and quantity of goods exchanged. A change in technology, such as a [[Technological_Advancement]] that enables firms to produce more efficiently, can shift the supply curve, potentially disrupting the equilibrium. The [[Market_Demand]] and [[Market_Equilibrium]] are closely related, as changes in demand can also impact the equilibrium price and quantity.

### Key Takeaways:

- A technological advancement can enable a firm to produce and supply more in the market.
- The market equilibrium is determined by the intersection of the demand and supply curves.
- Understanding market equilibrium is essential for analyzing the behavior of markets and making informed decisions.

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

However, real-world markets often face complications that can disrupt the equilibrium, such as changes in consumer preferences, government policies, or external shocks. Additionally, the assumption of perfect competition, which is necessary for market equilibrium to occur, is often not met in reality. Furthermore, the concept of market equilibrium assumes that markets clear instantly, which is not always the case. In reality, markets may take time to adjust to changes, and equilibrium may not always be achieved. The concept of market equilibrium relies heavily on the [[Law_Of_Supply]] and the [[Demand_Curve]].
