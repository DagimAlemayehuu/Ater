## 1. Mental Model

Think of your demand for a product as a rubber band. Some bands are stiff (inelastic) because you *must* have the product and have no other choice (e.g., insulin). Other bands are very stretchy (elastic) because if the price goes up, you can easily switch to something else or just wait (e.g., a specific brand of candy). These four determinants are the factors that decide how "stretchy" your demand rubber band is.

## 2. Causal Mechanism

The Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) is not constant for all goods; it depends on four primary factors that influence consumer behavior:

1.  **Availability of Substitutes**:
    - This is the **most important** determinant.
    - More substitutes = More elastic demand (stretchy).
    - If the price of oranges rises, you can easily switch to apples.
2.  **Time Period**:
    - Long run = More elastic demand.
    - In the short run, people are stuck with their habits. In the long run, they can find substitutes or change their lifestyle (e.g., buying a fuel-efficient car if gas prices stay high).
3.  **Proportion of Income Spent**:
    - Smaller proportion = Less elastic demand (stiff).
    - If the price of salt doubles, you barely notice because it's a tiny part of your budget. If the price of a car doubles, your demand will crash.
4.  **Nature of the Commodity (Luxury vs. Necessity)**:
    - **Necessities** (e.g., salt, basic food) tend to be **inelastic**. You need them regardless of price.
    - **Luxuries** (e.g., gold, designer bags) tend to be **elastic**. You can easily forego them if the price is too high.

### Key Takeaways:
- **Substitutes** and **Time** are the biggest drivers of elasticity.
- **Necessities** are "price-stiff," while **Luxuries** are "price-stretchy."

## 3. Limitations & Edge Cases

- **Definition of the Market**: The broader the definition, the more inelastic the demand. "Food" is very inelastic (no substitutes for food), but "Green Apples" are very elastic (many substitutes).
- **Addictive Goods**: Goods like cigarettes or certain medicines might have very few substitutes and be necessities for the user, making them extremely inelastic regardless of income proportion.

## 4. Determinants Matrix

| Factor | Condition | Elasticity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Type of Good** | Luxury | Elastic ($|E_p| > 1$) |
| **Type of Good** | Necessity | Inelastic ($|E_p| < 1$) |

## 5. Walkthrough

**Step 1:** Identify the good (e.g., Gasoline).
**Step 2:** Check substitutes: Few close substitutes for internal combustion engines (Inelastic).
**Step 3:** Check time: Short run (stuck with current car) vs. Long run (can buy EV) (Inelastic now, Elastic later).
**Step 4:** Check income share: Significant for most (Elastic).
**Step 5:** Check type: Necessity for commuting (Inelastic).
**Conclusion**: Gasoline demand is generally **inelastic** in the short run but becomes more **elastic** over time.
