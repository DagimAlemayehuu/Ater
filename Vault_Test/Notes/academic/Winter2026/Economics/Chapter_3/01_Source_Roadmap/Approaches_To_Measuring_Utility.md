## Mental Model
The concept of utility in economics refers to the satisfaction or pleasure a consumer derives from consuming a good or service. There are two major approaches to measuring utility: the Cardinal Approach and the Ordinal Approach.

## The Economic Intuition
The Cardinal Approach assumes that utility can be measured quantitatively, similar to how we measure weight or height. It assigns a specific numerical value to the utility a consumer derives from a good or service. On the other hand, the Ordinal Approach does not attempt to assign a numerical value to utility. Instead, it focuses on ranking preferences. Consumers can order their preferences from most to least desirable, but they do not assign specific numbers to each preference.

## The Calculation Logic
In the Cardinal Approach, utility is often measured in units called utils. For example, if a consumer derives 10 utils from consuming an apple and 20 utils from consuming an orange, the consumer prefers the orange. However, this approach has limitations, as it is difficult to accurately measure utility in numerical terms. The Ordinal Approach, in contrast, relies on indifference curves and budget constraints to analyze consumer preferences. By examining how consumers allocate their income across different goods and services, economists can infer their preferences.

## The Formal Math & Models
Mathematically, the Cardinal Approach can be represented using the utility function: $U = f(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$, where $U$ is the total utility and $x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$ are the quantities of each good or service consumed. The Ordinal Approach, on the other hand, uses indifference curves, which represent different levels of utility. The slope of the indifference curve is the marginal rate of substitution (MRS), which indicates how much of one good a consumer is willing to give up in exchange for another good.
