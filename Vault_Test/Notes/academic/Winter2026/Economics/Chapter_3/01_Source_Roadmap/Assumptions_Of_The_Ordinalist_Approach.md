## Mental Model
The ordinalist approach in economics is a way to understand consumer behavior. It is based on certain assumptions about how consumers make decisions.

## The Economic Intuition
The ordinalist approach assumes that consumers are rational beings who want to maximize their satisfaction. They make choices based on their preferences and the prices of goods and services. The goal is to get the most satisfaction possible with the money they have.

There are several key assumptions:
1. Consumers are rational and aim to maximize satisfaction.
2. Utility, or satisfaction, is ordinal, meaning it can be ranked but not precisely measured.
3. The marginal rate of substitution decreases, meaning that as a consumer gets more of one good, they are willing to give up less of another good to get even more of the first good.
4. Consumers make consistent decisions that align with their objectives.
5. The total satisfaction a consumer gets comes from all the items they consume.

## The Calculation Logic
The ordinalist approach uses a utility function to represent a consumer's preferences. This function shows how much satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming different combinations of goods and services. The function is written as \( U = f(x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n) \), where \( x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \) are the quantities of different goods and services.

The consumer is assumed to be at a point where they have not reached the maximum satisfaction for any good (not at a saturation point), so they prefer more of all goods.

## The Formal Math & Models
The utility function \( U = f(x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n) \) represents the total utility a consumer gets from consuming \( x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \) quantities of goods and services. This function is crucial for understanding how consumers make choices.

The diminishing marginal rate of substitution is a key concept. It can be represented using indifference curves, which show combinations of goods that give the same level of satisfaction. These curves are downward sloping and convex to the origin, reflecting the diminishing marginal rate of substitution.
