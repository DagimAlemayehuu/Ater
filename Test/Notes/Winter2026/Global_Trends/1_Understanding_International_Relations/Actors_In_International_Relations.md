## Mental Model

In a vast, intricate ecosystem, states are like coral reefs - each with their own unique structure and biodiversity, yet connected and influencing one another through the currents of international relations. Just as coral reefs provide a foundation for diverse marine life, states provide a foundation for various political entities to interact and thrive. As new "species" of political entities emerge, they often mimic the characteristics of established states, adopting similar features like defined territories, governments, and symbols of [[Sovereignty]].

## Global Strategy

**Actors in International Relations** refer to the various political entities that interact with each other on the global stage. These entities have evolved over time, and the traditional focus on interactions between states has been broadened to include relationships between all sorts of political entities, including international organizations, multinational corporations, and societies' citizens.

The underlying mechanism that defines **Actors in International Relations** is rooted in the concept of [[Sovereignty]] and the characteristics that come with it. A state, for instance, has a territorial extension, borders, a capital, an army, a foreign ministry, a flag, and a national anthem. All states claim to be sovereign, meaning they have the exclusive right to govern their respective territories in their own fashion. This **sovereignty** is a key factor in determining the relative influence of actors and shapes the structure of the [[International_System]]. The interactions between these actors, be they states or non-state entities, are what constitute international relations.

The real-world significance of Actors in International Relations lies in their impact on the global stage. The number of states, for example, has grown to 195, each with its own distinct characteristics. The influence of these actors and their interactions determines the dynamics of international politics, which can be thought of as a world stage where states are the leading actors. The distinction between international and domestic politics is also rooted in the concept of [[Sovereignty]], with international politics often characterized as anarchic. Ultimately, understanding the various Actors in International Relations is crucial for grasping the complexities of global politics and the [[Power_Dynamics]] at play.

## Diplomatic Friction

The concept of **Actors in International Relations** encompasses various political entities that interact on the global stage, influencing one another through complex [[Power_Dynamics]]. These entities, which include states, international organizations, and multinational corporations, operate within a framework defined by [[Sovereignty]] and international law. The interactions between these actors shape the structure of the [[International_System]], with states traditionally being the primary actors, exercising **sovereignty** and governing their territories. However, the landscape has evolved to include non-state actors, which have gained significance in international relations. Understanding these actors and their interactions is crucial for grasping the intricacies of global politics.

## Where It Breaks

| International Organizations | Entities formed by states to facilitate cooperation | Membership of states, defined purposes, structured governance |
| Multinational Corporations | Non-state actors with significant economic influence | Global operations, profit-driven, legal personality |
| Societies' Citizens | Individuals and groups influencing international relations | Participation in international interactions, impact on foreign policy |

**Incomplete Sovereignty**: Non-state actors may not possess full [[Sovereignty]], limiting their influence in international relations. **Unequal Power Dynamics**: The power imbalance between state and non-state actors can skew international interactions, favoring more powerful entities. **Evolving Actor Roles**: The roles and influence of different **actors in international relations** can change over time, challenging traditional understandings of global politics.
